Quest says patient records were reached through its ordering portal

Quest Diagnostics told the SEC that an unauthorised party accessed certain patient records in its Quanum LSM system, which clients use to order tests and look up results. It has not said how many patients are affected.

Sam Porter · House byline
October 23, 20262 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Quest Diagnostics, the US laboratory-testing company, said in a filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission dated Thursday that it learned on Wednesday of unauthorised access to its Quanum LSM system and believes an unauthorised party accessed certain patient records. The filing does not say how many patients are affected.

The 8-K describes Quanum LSM as a system "which certain clients use to place test orders, receive and look up results". Quest said it started its incident-response procedures to contain the activity, notified federal law enforcement and engaged outside cybersecurity experts. The full scope remains under investigation, the filing says, and the incident has not materially disrupted the company's operations or its ability to serve patients.

A notice on Quest's website, marked as of 08:00 Eastern on Friday, says the company paused access to Quanum Lab Services Manager on discovering the incident. Providers that order through direct laboratory-system interfaces or electronic medical records can carry on, and Quanum LSM customers can use paper requisitions or Quanum eLabs in the meantime. The notice says Quest anticipated restoring full service "as soon as" 23 October. Two other products, Essential Insights and LabHQ, were paused as a precaution; Quest says no data was accessed in them. It says it believes "some patient data within Quanum LSM was accessed" and is working to determine which data, and that the incident is not related to this week's PHP programming-language flaws.

Quest says it will notify affected patients, providers and health plans as the law requires, and that letters to patients will describe the resources available, including credit and identity monitoring. TechCrunch reported that Quest did not say when the intrusion began or whether attackers have demanded payment, and that a spokesperson, Amanda Rivera, did not answer its questions and pointed to the notice. TechCrunch also noted that the notice page carries code telling search engines not to list it.

It is not Quest's first exposure of patient data. In June 2019 the company disclosed that a breach at American Medical Collection Agency, a billing collections vendor, had reached information on about 11.9 million Quest patients; it said then that laboratory results were not affected. This time the system involved is Quest's own.

TechCrunch placed the incident in a run of healthcare attacks this year, including those at Stryker, Boston Scientific, CareCloud, Craneware and McKesson.

Why it matters: a laboratory ordering portal holds names, test orders and results for many practices at once, so one point of access can reach many providers' patients. So far the 8-K is the only source with a firm date and the company's own scope language. Komposite has reported on similar single-vendor exposure in the ConnectWise intrusions and Railway's call to rotate keys.
