What buyers actually do with an SBOM

Vendors have learned to produce the file. The harder question is what happens to it after it lands in a procurement inbox.

Sam Porter · Cybersecurity Reporter
August 30, 20262 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

The argument about whether vendors should produce a software bill of materials is over. Vendors produce them. The interesting question, and the one procurement teams are least prepared for, is what happens to the file after it arrives.

An SBOM is an inventory: the components inside a piece of software, their versions, and their relationships to one another. Its value is entirely conditional. It answers one question well, which is whether a newly disclosed vulnerability is present in something already installed, and it answers that question only if somebody is holding it when the disclosure lands.

The baseline moved this year. American authorities published an updated set of minimum elements, replacing guidance that had stood since the original executive order and raising what a compliant document is expected to contain.

That update matters more than it sounds, because the minimum elements are what contracts point at. A purchase order rarely specifies a format in detail. It references a standard, and the standard quietly determines how much work a vendor has to do.

Adoption across the European market has been surveyed rather than assumed. The EU cybersecurity agency's review of where SBOM practice actually stands describes an ecosystem where generation is increasingly routine and consumption remains the weak link.

That asymmetry is the whole story. Producing an SBOM is a build-pipeline problem, and build pipelines are the part of software organizations most amenable to automation. Consuming one is an operations problem, and operations teams are already the most oversubscribed function in most security programs.

The result is a familiar organizational pattern. A file arrives, satisfies a contractual requirement, and is stored where nothing queries it. The obligation is met and the risk is unchanged, which is the worst of both outcomes because it also produces a record showing the buyer was told.

Teams that get value from the practice describe the same prerequisite, and it is not tooling. It is knowing what software they run, at which version, in which environment, well enough to join that list against an inventory. Organizations that cannot answer the first question gain nothing from the second.

Regulatory drivers keep expanding the population who must care. Medical device guidance, the European cyber resilience regime and federal procurement terms each require the artifact in their own context, and each has a different theory of what the recipient is supposed to do with it.

Practitioners are candid that the honest use case is narrower than the marketing. When a widely used component is found to be catastrophically vulnerable, an SBOM turns a week of frantic archaeology into a database query. Between those events it is a maintenance cost.

That is a reasonable trade and a smaller claim than the one usually made for it. The organizations doing this well are not the ones with the best-formatted files. They are the ones who can answer, on the afternoon the disclosure lands, where the affected component is running.
