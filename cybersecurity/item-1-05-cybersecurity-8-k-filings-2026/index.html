Every Item 1.05 Cybersecurity 8-K Filed in 2026: 18 Companies, Days From Discovery to Filing and the Amendments

Eighteen listed companies have told the SEC this year that a cyber incident was material. Twelve are in health care, only two said their results took a material hit, and the median gap from detection to filing was nine and a half days.

Komposite News Staff
November 26, 202610 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Eighteen companies that report to the SEC have filed an Item 1.05 cybersecurity 8-K in 2026, the SEC form for a cyber incident a company has judged material, and where a filing dates the discovery, the median gap from detecting the incident to filing was nine and a half days. Twelve of the 18 are in health care or medical devices. Only Stryker and Boston Scientific said the incident had hit, or was likely to hit, their financial results. Nine said it was not likely to have a material financial impact at all, and seven had not yet worked it out when they filed.

The count is Komposite's, built from EDGAR full-text search and each company's own EDGAR filing list, with every filing read. No Item 1.05 report has been filed since Park Dental Partners updated its August incident on 13 October. Quest Diagnostics, which said on 22 October that an unauthorised party had reached patient records, used Item 8.01 instead and has filed nothing further on it.

The 18 companies that reported a material cyber incident in 2026

The SEC rule adopted in July 2023 requires a company to describe a material incident's nature, scope and timing, and its material or reasonably likely material impact. Most companies have had to comply since 18 December 2023, and smaller reporting companies since 15 June 2024. The record below lists the 2026 reports in the order they were filed.

How long companies took from detection to an Item 1.05 filing

Fourteen filings give a detection date. Measured from that date, the fastest was The Oncology Institute, two days after a vendor's administrator told it the breach had reached its systems. The slowest were Stryker at 29 days and Navient at 24. Navient's delay has a plain explanation in its filing: the ransomware hit a law firm it uses, and Navient judged the matter material three weeks after it learned of it.

The deadline does not run from detection. The rule gives a company four business days from the date it decides an incident is material, and tells it to make that decision without unreasonable delay. Ten filings state that decision date, and all ten met the four-day clock, most with two days to spare.

Analysis: the rule's real pressure point is the materiality decision, which companies control and mostly do not date. Eight of the 18 filings give no date for it. Nothing in the rule makes them, so a reader cannot tell from those filings whether a decision came the day after detection or three weeks later.

Most filers said the incident would not hurt their finances

Half the 2026 filers called an incident material while saying it was not likely to have a material financial impact. That is not a contradiction under the rule. The SEC wrote in its adopting release that companies should weigh qualitative factors, and named harm to reputation, customer relationships and the possibility of litigation or regulatory action. iRhythm said it judged its incident material in light of the volume of potentially affected data. Amgen gave the same reason, citing the volume of files taken from cloud environments run by third parties.

The two companies that did report a hit to results are the largest operational disruptions in the set. Stryker's amendment said the incident had a material impact on its operations and on its first-quarter results, but not on its full-year guidance. Its first-quarter release showed organic sales growth of 2.4 per cent, against full-year guidance of 8.0 to 9.5 per cent that it kept. Boston Scientific said in its 8 September filing that it was unlikely to meet its guidance. Its third-quarter release reported net sales of $5.138bn, up 1.5 per cent, after growth of 7.5 per cent in the second quarter. Neither company has put a dollar figure on its incident, and Boston Scientific's 10-Q filed on 2 November still lists the incident among its risks.

Four companies reported under another item first

In May 2024 the director of the SEC's Division of Corporation Finance, Erik Gerding, asked companies to use a different item, such as Item 8.01, for incidents that were immaterial or not yet assessed. If such an incident is later judged material, the statement says, an Item 1.05 report should follow within four business days of that decision.

Four of this year's filers followed that path. Stryker reported its incident under Item 8.01 on the day it found it and moved it to Item 1.05 by amendment 29 days later. Boston Scientific's 8.01 report came a day after detection and its Item 1.05 report 13 days after that. Nutex Health said in its 24 August 8.01 report that it did not believe the incident was likely to have a material impact. Seven days later it filed under Item 1.05, and that filing referred to litigation over the breach. The Oncology Institute first described its vendor's incident in an Item 7.01 report in November 2025, six months before its Item 1.05 filing.

Health care companies filed most of this year's reports

Twelve of the 18 are health care providers, medical device makers, drug makers or health software firms: UFP Technologies, CareCloud, Stryker, West Pharmaceutical, The Oncology Institute, iRhythm, AdaptHealth, Amgen, Nutex, Park Dental, Boston Scientific and Astrana. Four are in finance (two banks, the loan servicer Navient and the bitcoin ATM operator Bitcoin Depot) and two are technology companies. Komposite reported in October that Quest Diagnostics' patient records were reached through its ordering portal, the same pattern outside the Item 1.05 list.

The way in was often someone else's system. Six filings trace the incident to a vendor, contractor, law firm, integration or third-party cloud: The Oncology Institute, iRhythm, 8x8, AdaptHealth, Navient and Amgen. Three describe social engineering, including Astrana's account of callers who spoofed the company's main phone number. CB Financial Services stands apart. Its bank subsidiary found that non-public customer information, including Social Security numbers, had been handled in an unauthorised AI-based software application. No attacker is named, and the bank said there was no disruption to its operations.

Item 1.05 filings by year since the rule took effect

By our count, 2026 has already produced more original Item 1.05 reports than all of 2025, though from fewer companies than 2024. Analysis: the fall after 2024 matches the timing of the May 2024 staff statement, which discouraged using Item 1.05 for incidents not yet judged material.

Amendments are rarer than the rule implies. Its Instruction 2 requires an amended 8-K once information that was missing at filing becomes known. Seven of this year's filers said some impact was not yet determined. Apart from Stryker, whose amendment was its first Item 1.05 report, only West Pharmaceutical and River Financial have filed Item 1.05 amendments so far, River four times in July. Its last amendment still had not said whether the incident was material. Nutex and Park Dental updated through a press release and a second 8-K instead.

How to check a company's cyber filings on EDGAR

  1. Open EDGAR full-text search and search the exact phrase "Item 1.05" with the form type set to 8-K, which also returns 8-K/A amendments.
  2. Run the company name a second time with "cybersecurity incident" and no item filter, to catch Item 8.01 and 7.01 reports filed before or instead of an Item 1.05 report.
  3. In each filing, note three dates: when the incident was found, when the company judged it material, and when it filed.
  4. Check the company's filing list for 8-K/A amendments and for the next 10-Q, which may describe costs the 8-K left out.

Mistakes to avoid when reading Item 1.05 filings

The first is reading "material" as a statement about money. Most 2026 filers used the item while saying their finances were not likely to be materially affected. The second is measuring the deadline from the breach. It runs from the materiality decision, which many filings do not date. The third is treating the Item 1.05 list as the full record of serious incidents. Quest's patient-record breach and every 8.01-only disclosure sit outside it. Banks also answer to a separate regime: Komposite explained in November how the bank incident notice rule gives a bank 36 hours to tell its regulator.

Whether the SEC will keep the rule

Item 1.05 is unchanged, but it is under review. A joint petition filed in 2025 asks the SEC to rescind it. In January the SEC chairman, Paul Atkins, directed a review of Regulation S-K, aimed at dropping requirements that elicit immaterial information, and opened file CLL-15 for comment. In a 2 October letter to that file, the wireless trade group CTIA asked the SEC to rescind the Item 1.05 requirement, arguing that its narrow delay allowance for national security does not fit the way the FBI coordinates incident responses. Komposite found no SEC proposal to change it. Until it does, the next test of the four-day clock is the next company to decide an incident is material, and Quest Diagnostics is the most recent large disclosure still filed under Item 8.01.
