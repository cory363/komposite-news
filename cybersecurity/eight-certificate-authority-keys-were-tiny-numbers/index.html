Eight certificate keys that were tiny numbers

Eight eMudhra intermediate certificates had private keys below 256, recoverable almost instantly. Browsers have blocked them and Mozilla is pulling the root, but software that does not check revocation can still be fooled by forgeries.

Sam Porter · House byline
October 21, 20263 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Eight intermediate certificates issued by eMudhra, an Indian certificate authority, had private keys so small that anyone could recover them, letting an attacker issue trusted HTTPS certificates for any website, Mozilla said on Tuesday. A certificate authority vouches for websites' identities; its private key is what makes that vouching unforgeable.

The keys were integers below 256. "The private keys are effectively all-zeroes apart from the last byte," Dennis Jackson of Mozilla wrote on Mozilla's public bug tracker; a P-384 key should be a random number of up to 384 bits. He added that local AI agents he tested "were able to recover the private key on their first attempt with a simplistic prompt". Six of the certificates are for web (TLS) traffic and trusted by Chrome, Mozilla, Microsoft and Apple; two are for code signing and trusted by Microsoft.

Security researcher Scott Helme wrote that he recovered all eight on his laptop, including one that is simply the number 4, by trying at most 255 candidates. The certificates were created on 30 April 2025, which Helme called "a 17 month window" in which anyone examining them could have done the same. Mozilla's post does not report any misuse, and CoinDesk said no crypto attacks had been reported.

The response was fast. Mozilla received the report, forwarded by Chrome's root programme, at 20:06 UTC on Saturday 17 October, and had pushed a block through OneCRL, its central revocation list, by 22:59 UTC. It released NSS 3.131.1, its certificate library, removing the root that signed the intermediates, and Firefox 158.0.1 with ESR 115.43.1 and 153.5.1; a further Android release followed at 08:29 UTC on Wednesday. Chrome blocked the certificates the same day, Apple on 19 October and Microsoft on 20 October, CoinDesk reported.

eMudhra's account is on the same bug. It revoked the eight on 18 October and said the B and C series, built for its US and European operations, "were not put into production except" one, from which no end-entity certificates were issued. On Monday it said a preliminary review found "a flaw in the KGC script", its key-generation ceremony, which created a second, weaker key pair that was used "by mistake" because the key labels were similar. "There were human errors in multiple steps of the process," it wrote. The Register said eMudhra, which says it has issued more than 60 million certificates, had not answered its questions.

Mozilla then went further. Citing multiple people recovering the keys independently, it announced the emergency removal of trust in eMudhra's emSign ECC Root CA - G3 to protect software that cannot use revocation. eMudhra replied on the bug that it had ceased issuance under that root, and that about 30,000 certificates, about 97 per cent of them obtained through the automated ACME protocol, need migrating to its RSA root.

The remaining exposure is outside browsers. Helme wrote that most non-browser clients trust a certificate bundle stored on disk and do not check revocation by default; in his tests curl, Python and Node accepted a forged certificate for api.stripe.com on every Linux and macOS machine he tried, and Windows accepted the chain unless revocation checking was switched on. Devices with frozen firmware, such as routers and printers, "will trust these certificates until expiry, which is July 2039," he wrote.

Cloudflare, quoted by The Register, said "an attacker can create a forged certificate for any domain", though the attacker would still need to sit between the user and the server. CoinDesk noted that exchange bots, trading scripts and wallet back ends often run on Python, Node or Go. "This is not a crypto-specific issue," Mudit Gupta, chief technology officer of Polygon Labs, told the outlet. "It is a generic issue that can be exploited for man-in-the-middle attacks."

Why it matters: the browser programmes closed the hole within hours, but revocation only protects software that checks it, so the fix elsewhere is updating certificate bundles and rebuilding container images.
