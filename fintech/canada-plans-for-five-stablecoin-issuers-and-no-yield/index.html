Canada plans for five stablecoin issuers, and no yield

Canada's Department of Finance has published draft rules for its Stablecoin Act and opened a 60-day consultation. Issuers would register with the Bank of Canada, keep short-dated reserves with a Canadian custodian and pay holders no interest. Ottawa expects about five issuers.

Elena Vasquez · House byline
October 26, 20263 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Canada would bar stablecoin issuers from paying interest or yield to holders, directly or through third parties, under draft regulations the Department of Finance published in the Canada Gazette on Saturday. The proposals implement the Stablecoin Act, which received royal assent in March. They are open for comment for 60 days, until 23 December by our count, and the government intends them to take effect in the autumn of 2027.

The prohibition is drawn widely. Issuers may not use terms or symbols suggesting that they would, directly or indirectly, "pay the holder of a stablecoin any form of interest or yield from holding the stablecoin", and may not enter arrangements, including income-sharing ones, that encourage people to hold more of a token or hold it for longer. A reward is permitted only where it is "tied to only the use and not the holding of a stablecoin", according to the notice. The regulatory text sets out when a payment under an arrangement with another party counts as "indirect granting or payment of an interest or yield".

The reserve rules are conservative. Issuers that are not already prudentially regulated financial institutions must register with the Bank of Canada, and at the end of each day must hold reserves in the reference currency with a market value at least equal to the tokens outstanding. Eligible assets are limited to government debt maturing in three months or less, demand deposits, one-business-day reverse repurchase agreements on that debt, and money market funds invested only in such assets. Reserves cannot be pledged as collateral and must sit with a qualified custodian incorporated in Canada, in a segregated, bankruptcy-remote account. Issuers may withdraw returns from the reserve as long as it stays at or above par.

Holders must be able to redeem one for one, with no minimum amount, within three business days of a request. Redemption fees are capped at 1 per cent of the amount redeemed, and an issuer must give seven days' notice on its website before raising them; the notice says current fees are about 0.1 per cent. Applicants also face a national security review: the Minister of Finance has 60 days to decide whether to review an application, and a review runs for 180 days, both periods being extendable. The Governor of the Bank of Canada may exempt issuers whose existing requirements are "substantially similar" to the act's.

Ottawa does not expect a crowded market. The department's cost-benefit analysis assumes five issuers, based on current players including QCAD from Stablecorp, CADC from Loon and Circle's USDC, plus potential entrants. It puts their administrative and compliance costs at C$88,521 a year, or C$17,704 per issuer. Including changes to payment service provider rules that would bring about 30 more firms into scope, the total annualised cost is C$240,648. Separately, the Bank of Canada would recover its supervisory costs through annual assessments: a base fee of C$750,000 for issuers with C$1bn or more outstanding, C$350,000 for those with at least C$500m and C$25,000 for smaller ones, plus a variable charge for the two larger tiers.

Canada's banks have argued for the yield ban. A spokesperson for the Canadian Bankers Association told The Logic in June that the group "firmly supports the Stablecoin Act's explicit prohibition on yield, both direct and indirect", CoinDesk reported. The same report noted that the Ontario Securities Commission allowed Coinbase Canada this year to offer yields on eligible tokens, subject to risk warnings, and that Canada's six largest banks said last month they were exploring a tokenised system for moving Canadian dollars between financial institutions.

Why it matters: rules on whether stablecoin holders can earn a return are being written in several jurisdictions at once, and the answer shapes whether the tokens compete with bank deposits. Canada's draft bans returns on holding while leaving rewards for use open. Komposite reported last week that the OCC finalised its GENIUS Act rule, effective 18 January 2027, dropping a presumption against rewards and leaving yield undefined. In September Canada's banking supervisor said tokenised deposits are not legally distinct from ordinary deposits. Comments on the draft go to Gloria Wong, director of digital assets at the Department of Finance.
