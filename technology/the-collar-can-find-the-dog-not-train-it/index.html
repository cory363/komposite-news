The collar can find the dog, not train it

GPS and LTE collars from Fi, Halo and Tractive now promise live location, virtual fences and health alerts for a monthly fee. Remote training collars remain contested. Joe Cinnante, founder of the Los Angeles trainer Cinnante Canine, says the results still come from experienced people.

Tom Kessler · Enterprise Technology Reporter
October 22, 20265 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

A dog collar in 2026 can report its location over a cellular network, draw a virtual fence and flag unusual scratching, but it cannot teach a recall, according to the makers' own pages. Fi, Halo and Tractive sell GPS trackers on subscription. Cinnante Canine, a Los Angeles-area training company, offers what the hardware cannot: experienced trainers. Its board-and-train programme comes in two tiers, one led by its founder, Joe Cinnante, and one led by his team, the company's site says.

The hardware is specific. Halo, which sells GPS fence collars, lists its Halo Collar 5 at $524, down from $599, on its home page. It claims GPS accuracy of "under 2ft", unlimited virtual fences stored on the collar so they work without signal, and up to 48 hours of battery. A Pack Membership, which Halo says is required to activate GPS, cellular service and fences, starts at $9.99 a month. Fi, whose collar is the Series 3+, claims "up to 3 months" of battery on its site and says every membership includes the collar itself. Fi's pages did not show a price when Komposite read them. Tractive lists its DOG 6 at $79 with up to two weeks of battery, and says it works in "175+ countries" over 2G or LTE networks.

The subscription is where the cost sits. Tractive's plans page starts at $108 billed annually, and lists 365-day location history and heart and respiratory rate monitoring under its Premium tier, against 24 hours of history for Basic users. By our arithmetic, a Halo Collar 5 on its entry plan costs about $644 in its first year, and a Tractive DOG 6 on the cheapest annual plan costs $187. Both figures are before any discount for multi-year terms. The makers' prices are their own, and Komposite did not test the devices.

Health tracking is the newer pitch, and the makers hedge it. Fi says its collar tracks rest, eating, drinking, scratching, licking and barking, and that its AI can catch early signs of "allergies or discomfort". Tractive offers "daily health insights like vitals, activity and sleep", plus bark monitoring that it says can "detect signs of separation anxiety". Its own footnote says: "Tractive is not a medical device." None of the three companies claims its collar diagnoses illness. They describe changes in behaviour that an owner, or a vet, is then expected to interpret.

Training is where the devices meet the argument. Halo's collar can deliver sound, vibration and what it calls "optional static levels", described on its feedback page as a "gentle tap on the shoulder". Halo says "Static is never required" and that "Halo Collar is not a shock collar", distinguishing its feedback from "a single high-voltage electric pulse" in traditional devices. Its boundary training programme was "Developed with Cesar Millan". Halo's own FAQ says the owner, not the collar, should do the training. Those are Halo's descriptions and were not independently verified.

Hand-held remote training collars, or e-collars, divide professional bodies. The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior (AVSAB) says, in its 2021 position statement as published on its site, that "There is no evidence that aversive methods are more effective than reward-based methods in any context", and lists electronic shock collars among aversive tools. The Certification Council for Professional Dog Trainers (CCPDT), which certifies trainers, takes a different line. Its position statement says it "does not forbid the use of electronic collars" used within its "Humane Hierarchy", but that a certificant "should never" use electric stimulation "as an initial training option".

The most-cited research comes from the University of Lincoln. In a 2020 paper in Frontiers in Veterinary Science, Lucy China, Daniel Mills and Jonathan Cooper compared 63 dogs with recall and chasing problems across three groups of 21. Dogs trained by reward-focused trainers responded better to "Sit" and "Come" after a single command than dogs trained with active e-collars, and the authors found "no evidence to indicate that E-collar training is necessary". They also noted limits: the video came from a 2010-11 government project, groups trained in different seasons, and methods differed beyond the collar itself.

The law is unsettled even where it has moved. Michael Gove, then Environment Secretary, announced in August 2018 that remote-controlled e-collars for dogs and cats would be banned in England, while exempting invisible fences. Draft regulations set a start date of 1 February 2024, and Parliament's Petitions Committee, rejecting a petition in March 2025, said they "came into effect on February 1, 2024". The official register does not show that. legislation.gov.uk still lists the Animal Welfare (Electronic Collars) (England) Regulations 2023 as draft legislation that "has not yet been made as a UK Statutory Instrument". The Royal Kennel Club, which supports a total ban, says the use and sale of shock collars "is currently not prohibited in England". Wales has banned them since 2010.

That is the gap Joe Cinnante has built his business around. A trainer with more than 25 years of experience, he has trained and provided dogs for military and police K9 units, founded Advanced Canine Systems in 2013 and launched Cinnante Canine in 2023, according to his About page. The company's promise is two words: "Real Results." It is pitched at owners who have already paid for something that did not work. "Every day, dog owners invest thousands into training programs that fail to deliver," Cinnante said in a statement to Komposite News.

The model is people rather than hardware. "At Cinnante Canine, your dog's training is never outsourced. Every dog is trained in-house by our team of experts," Cinnante said. The direction is upmarket. The company already works for "a number of very high net worth clients", he said, adding: "Basically what we want to move towards is high end / high level care for affluent clients and their dogs."

Cinnante uses technology where it helps owners follow the work. During a retreat, the company says on its website, "We provide regular updates on your dog's progress, including photos and videos." Its training page offers one-on-one virtual sessions alongside private lessons around Los Angeles, and an online programme with training videos and live Q&A is listed as "COMING SOON!".

The spending is real. The American Pet Products Association puts actual US spending on pets at $158 billion in its latest figures and projects $165 billion for 2026, with training counted inside a $14.3bn "Other Services" line that also covers boarding, grooming, insurance and pet sitting. The collar answers where the dog is, and a growing share of the time how it is. Whether it comes when called is still, on the makers' own account and on Cinnante's, a matter of training. His summary of what owners are paying for is four short sentences: "Better behavior. More trust. More freedom. Less stress."
