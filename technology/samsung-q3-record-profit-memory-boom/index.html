Samsung Made $80 Billion in a Quarter. Its Phones Lost Money.

The world's largest memory chipmaker posted the biggest quarterly profit in tech history on AI demand, even as its smartphone business sank into deficit and it cut production by 30 percent.

Alison Acosta · Senior Technology Correspondent
October 8, 202620 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Samsung Electronics expects to report an operating profit of about 107.4 trillion won, roughly $80.17 billion, for the third quarter. That is nearly nine times what it earned a year earlier, the fourth straight quarter of record profit, and, by the company's own telling, the first time any technology company has cleared 100 trillion won in a single quarter.

Reuters reported that the figure landed slightly ahead of the LSEG SmartEstimate of 106.1 trillion won, as booming demand for AI chips drove memory sales to levels that would have sounded fantastical two years ago. DRAM margins are now estimated to run above 80 percent. The product doing most of the work is high-bandwidth memory, the stacked chips that sit beside GPUs and feed them data fast enough for AI training, and Samsung has already begun selling HBM4 to Nvidia, with HBM4E reportedly clearing Nvidia's quality approval for the Rubin Ultra platform.

None of this happened because Galaxy phones sold better. It happened because the chips inside everyone else's data centers got scarce, and scarce memory is suddenly the most profitable thing Samsung makes.

The most profitable company in tech can't make money on phones

Here is the paradox inside the record. While the chip division prints money, Samsung's mobile business, once its crown jewel, is losing it. Brokerages estimate the mobile and networks businesses lost between 900 billion won and 1.9 trillion won in the quarter, deeper than the 700 billion won deficit of the second quarter, which was itself the division's first-ever quarterly loss. Sammobile put the mobile operating loss near $1.11 billion, with the foundry and System LSI businesses losing a combined $743 million on top of that.

The culprit is the same boom feeding the record. Memory prices are skyrocketing so fast that they are eating the phone business alive. Samsung raised prices on the seven-month-old Galaxy S26 lineup by nearly 150,000 won in Korea and $100 to $200 in the United States this month, a reversal from July, when co-CEO Roh Tae-moon told reporters the company would not pass the full cost increase to consumers. The Z Fold 8 drew a record 1.44 million preorders in Korea, and it still was not enough. The Investor's analysis put the dilemma bluntly: with shortages expected to persist into 2027, there is no clear path back to phone profitability without pushing prices higher still.

Samsung's answer has been to make fewer phones. MoneyToday reported the company plans to cut fourth-quarter smartphone production by 30 percent, roughly 70 million fewer units for the year, and has asked component partners to reduce deliveries by 20 to 30 percent. When memory is worth more in a data center than in a handset, the rational move is to feed the data center.

The market is not celebrating

Samsung shares slipped 0.2 percent on the news, underperforming a soft Kospi, and have now fallen more than 25 percent from their June record. The market's focus, as Mirae Asset's Kim Seok-hwan told Reuters, has shifted to whether growth this violent can be sustained. Analysts expect fourth-quarter profit growth to slow to 8.2 percent sequentially, down from 20 percent in the third quarter, as chip price gains abate.

The risks are real. Chinese competition is intensifying, a potential slowdown in AI spending hangs over every forecast, and the won's sharp appreciation is trimming the value of dollar-denominated sales. Samsung and Micron both expect the memory imbalance to persist into 2028, but as the memory industry learned in every previous cycle, scarcity pricing invites exactly the capacity investment that eventually ends it.

For now, though, the numbers are the numbers. A company that lost money selling phones just posted the largest quarterly profit any tech company has ever reported, because the world cannot get enough of the memory inside its chips. The AI boom has a way of producing sentences like that, and Samsung's third quarter may be the strangest one yet.
