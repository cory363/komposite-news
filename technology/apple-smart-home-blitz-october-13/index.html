Apple Is Coming for Your Front Door

A doorbell, a deadbolt, thermostats and cameras, built with LG and launching October 13. It is Apple's first full-scale assault on the smart home, and the first big product push of the John Ternus era.

Alison Acosta · Senior Technology Correspondent
October 8, 202622 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Apple is about to pick a fight it has avoided for a decade. The company is preparing its most direct challenge yet to Amazon and Google in the connected home, lining up a new hub and a slate of security and climate devices developed with LG Electronics, with an introduction targeted for October 13.

The products include a doorbell, a deadbolt lock, a thermostat, an indoor camera, an outdoor camera and a floodlight camera, all designed around an Apple smart-home ecosystem the company has been quietly rebuilding for years, Bloomberg News reported. The hardware is expected to arrive alongside a long-delayed smart-home hub, an updated HomePod mini and a new Apple TV.

If the schedule holds, it will be Apple's first full-scale push into a category where Amazon's Ring and Alphabet's Nest have spent years entrenching themselves with doorbells, cameras, locks and thermostats. It will also be one of the first major product expansions under Chief Executive John Ternus, and a formal reboot of HomeKit, the platform Apple introduced in 2014 that never matched the reach of Alexa or Google Home.

The hub is the tell

At the center of the effort is a tabletop hub, internally code-named J490: a roughly six-inch square display on a tiltable arm, described as reminiscent of the early-2000s iMac G4. A wall-mountable variant, J491, is also in development. The device runs a new interface drawn from iOS, tvOS and watchOS, built around Apple's Siri AI, and is expected to handle device control, FaceTime, intercom, media and household personalization, using its front camera and voice recognition to switch the screen to whoever is standing in front of it.

The design language matters. Apple is not building another puck or cylinder to hide on a shelf. A tilting display with a camera that recognizes household members is a statement that the company thinks the smart home's center of gravity should be a screen, not a speaker, and that it should know who it is talking to. Amazon's Echo Show and Google's Nest Hub have gestured at this for years without making it feel essential. Apple is betting its industrial design and chip advantage can do what theirs could not.

Why now, and why LG

The LG partnership is the pragmatic core of the plan. Apple has world-class silicon, software and retail, but it does not manufacture doorbells or thermostats at scale, and building that muscle alone would take years. LG brings appliance manufacturing depth and a complementary interest in the connected home. For Apple, it is a way to arrive with a full shelf of products on day one rather than a single hero device surrounded by promises.

The timing is also about the person at the top. Ternus, who took over as chief executive, needs a growth story beyond the iPhone, and the smart home is one of the few consumer categories where Apple has genuine headroom. The company will report fourth-quarter earnings on November 2, with analysts already watching for commentary on memory pricing and supply constraints. A successful October 13 launch would give Ternus something more valuable than a good quarter: a second act narrative heading into the holidays, just as the iPhone Duo foldable launches on October 23.

A decade of false starts

This is not Apple's first attempt at the home. HomeKit launched in 2014 to grand promises of a Siri-controlled house, then spent years as a checkbox feature, hamstrung by strict certification requirements that kept device makers at arm's length while Alexa and Google Home raced ahead on openness. Apple later backed the Matter standard, the industry's attempt at a common smart-home language, but Matter's rollout has been slower and messier than its backers promised, leaving the market fragmented.

The new lineup reads as Apple's verdict that standards were not enough and that it needs to own the hardware itself. A first-party doorbell and deadbolt, sold in Apple Stores and installed by Apple-trained technicians, would do for the entryway what the Apple Watch did for the wrist: make the category feel like it was designed rather than assembled. The risk is the one Apple always runs when it enters late, that the market has already decided, and that Ring's millions of doorbells and Nest's years of data are a moat no amount of aluminum and glass can cross in a single product cycle.

Ring and Nest should be nervous, but not panicked. Both have vast installed bases, subscription revenue and years of refinement. What they do not have is Apple's ability to make a doorbell feel like it belongs in the same design universe as everything else in your life. On October 13, we find out whether that is enough.
