Musk closes a loophole Kyiv says never existed

Elon Musk said at the weekend that SpaceX restricted Starlink in Ukraine because its military broke the service's terms by using it for drone strikes. Ukrainian officials said that was technically impossible. Speeds were restored on Saturday.

Tom Kessler · House byline
October 26, 20263 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Elon Musk said SpaceX restricted Starlink in Ukraine last week because Ukraine's military had broken the satellite service's terms by using it for offensive drone strikes, NBC News reported, a claim Ukrainian officials said was technically impossible. In posts on X from Saturday night into Sunday, the SpaceX chief executive wrote that "That loophole was closed last week" and that "We simply enforced our terms of service when they were bypassed."

The posts came after Ukraine said its service was back to normal. Ukraine's Defence Ministry said on Saturday that connections were stable again at more than 400 kilometres per hour, the Kyiv Independent reported, after speeds on Starshield, the military version of the network, had been capped at about 161 km/h since mid-October. President Volodymyr Zelensky said: "The speeds are now where they need to be." The cap matters because drones and interceptors fly faster than that. Ukrainian officials said the restrictions were imposed without warning, hampered drone interception and forced some operations to be cancelled, according to NBC.

Mr Musk wrote that Ukraine's military "violated Starlink terms of service and used it for offensive drone strikes to kill people", and that "Were Starlink to be turned off, the Ukrainian front line would collapse immediately." He also said SpaceX had done more than any other company to help Ukraine, the Kyiv Independent reported. Earlier in the dispute he had written that for Starshield "any limits on speed or usage are decided by the US military and ultimately POTUS", according to NBC. SpaceX did not immediately respond to NBC's request for comment.

Kyiv's rebuttal rests on how the civilian network is already configured. Serhii Beskrestnov, a defence technology adviser to Mr Zelensky, said civilian Starlink in Ukraine is limited to speeds of 70 to 90 km/h and does not work over Russian territory. "Therefore, we physically cannot use commercial Starlink for deep strikes or medium-range strikes," he wrote on Telegram, the Associated Press reported. Serhii Sternenko, a former adviser to the Defence Ministry on drones, said such use was "technically impossible" and that Ukraine "did not violate the terms of use", according to NBC. In a post on X reported by the Kyiv Independent, he said whitelists introduced in February meant any terminal used on the move had to be registered and connected to Starshield.

That leaves the two sides describing different systems. Mr Musk's terms-of-service argument concerns commercial Starlink, which SpaceX controls. Ukraine says the cap that hit its operations was on Starshield, which Mr Musk has said is for the US government to limit. Which network was throttled, and on whose instruction, has not been independently established. The Wall Street Journal reported that President Donald Trump told Mr Musk on 21 October to lift the restrictions, according to the Kyiv Independent. Asked whether he had spoken to Mr Musk on Thursday, Mr Trump said: "The bottom line — he put it back," NBC reported. Senator Richard Blumenthal opened an inquiry on Friday asking the Pentagon who ordered the limits.

For governments the episode is a lesson in single-vendor dependence. Moritz Brake, a senior fellow at the Center for Advanced Security, a Washington think tank, said Mr Musk's ability to limit the network gives him "enormous leverage", according to NBC. Ukraine's Defence Ministry announced last week a partnership with Nokia, the Finnish telecoms equipment maker, and ICEYE, a Finnish satellite imagery company, to develop what it called a "sovereign satellite communications" system, NBC reported. No timetable or cost was given in the reports. It is not the first interruption: NBC noted that service also dropped during a Ukrainian push to retake territory in September 2022.

Why it matters: low-earth-orbit connectivity has become infrastructure for armies as well as businesses, and this episode shows how one operator's terms of service, or a government's instruction to that operator, can change what a customer can do overnight. Procurement teams buying satellite links, which carriers now resell alongside wireline as a standard option, have reason to read the terms closely. Komposite reported last week that SpaceX and Tesla had formed special committees to weigh a merger, as SpaceX reported $14.6bn of third-quarter revenue.
