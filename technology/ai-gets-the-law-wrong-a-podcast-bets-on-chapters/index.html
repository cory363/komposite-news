AI gets the law wrong. A podcast bets on chapters

Chatbots still garble legal detail. Two Taps and Friends, a show hosted by criminal defense lawyers, structures every episode so search engines can find the lawyers' own explanation rather than a summary of it.

Tom Kessler · House byline
October 28, 20263 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Studies keep finding that AI assistants get legal and news detail wrong, and one podcast hosted by criminal defense lawyers is answering with metadata. Two Taps and Friends marks up its episodes with chapters, transcripts and clip data so that search engines point people to the lawyers' explanation itself.

The accuracy problem is well documented. Researchers writing for Stanford's Institute for Human-Centered AI reported hallucination rates of 69% to 88% when they put specific legal queries to GPT 3.5, Llama 2 and PaLM 2, in a study published in January 2024. Those models have since been replaced, but the pattern has carried into news. A study of more than 3,000 answers from ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini and Perplexity, coordinated by the European Broadcasting Union and led by the BBC, found that "45% of all AI answers had at least one significant issue", VRT reported in October 2025. In criminal law the detail that gets dropped is often the one that matters: whether a charge came by information or by indictment, or whether a federal count was dismissed rather than tried.

Two Taps and Friends is a weekly show on YouTube and the podcast apps. Daniel Rosenberg, who runs a criminal defense practice in Fort Lauderdale, Florida, hosts it with fellow defense attorneys Michael Dye and Lloyd Golburgh. Its Legal Panel episodes explain trials "the way working defense lawyers see them", the show's site says.

Google says creators can steer this. Its video guidance states: "We will prioritize key moments set by you, either through structured data or the YouTube description." Two Taps titles many of its chapters after the rule in dispute rather than the headline. On episode 94, about the search of Representative Ilhan Omar's home, the chapters cover probable cause, how far a warrant for phones reaches and grand jury subpoenas. On episode 84, about Luigi Mangione, they cover state versus federal charges and New York's double jeopardy rule. Someone searching for either rule can land on the minute where a lawyer explains it.

The transcripts carry the reasoning verbatim. "Now, what I will say is to get a search warrant is not so easy, right?" Rosenberg says in the episode 94 transcript. "The standard is not the same as of a conviction. Conviction is beyond a reasonable doubt. The standard is probable cause for a search warrant, right?"

Clips are the risky layer. A 60-second cut can reach people who will never sit through 48 minutes, and it can also lose the caveat. The chapters and transcripts work as the backstop, because each clip points back to a full episode where the argument is on the record. "People search for a case, not for a podcast. When someone looks up a trial, we want a defense lawyer's explanation to be what they find," Rosenberg said. "A headline can tell you someone was charged. It can't tell you whether the charge was brought the right way, and that is often where a case is won or lost," he added.

The audience is there to be found. YouTube said in February 2025 that it had more than 1 billion monthly active viewers of podcast content. Rosenberg ends episodes with the creator's usual request to subscribe, because "it builds the channel, helps us more outreach, get more topics, get more guests". The show says nothing on it is legal advice. New full episodes usually arrive on Wednesdays, the show says, on its YouTube channel.
