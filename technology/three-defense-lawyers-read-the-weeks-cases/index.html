Three defense lawyers read the week's cases

Two Taps and Friends puts criminal defense lawyers in front of the week's legal stories, from the Ilhan Omar search to Luigi Mangione's double jeopardy question. Then it chapters, transcribes and clips the result so it surfaces where people search.

Tom Kessler · House byline
October 28, 20265 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Two Taps and Friends, a weekly podcast on YouTube, has three working defense lawyers, Daniel Rosenberg, Michael Dye and Lloyd Golburgh, explain the week's big cases, from the Ilhan Omar search to Luigi Mangione's double jeopardy question. Its Legal Panel episodes take the trials in the news and explain them "the way working defense lawyers see them", the show's site says. Rosenberg hosts every episode. Dye is described there as "Daniel's trial partner", and Golburgh has "more than three decades of legal experience".

The latest episode shows the method. Federal agents searched Ilhan Omar's Washington home on 12 October and grand jury subpoenas went to some of her relatives, the show's newsroom reported, citing the Associated Press and CBS News. She has not been charged and denies wrongdoing. On episode 94, released on 21 October, the panel set the headline aside and started with the paperwork: what agents had to show a judge, how far a warrant for phones reaches, and what subpoenas to family members say about where prosecutors are going. "Now, what I will say is to get a search warrant is not so easy, right?" Rosenberg says in the episode's transcript. "The standard is not the same as of a conviction. Conviction is beyond a reasonable doubt. The standard is probable cause for a search warrant, right?"

Procedure is where the panel spends its time. In episode 84, Rosenberg and Dye walked through the Mangione case on two tracks: second-degree murder in New York state court, and a federal case in which the murder count had been dismissed, leaving stalking charges. The question the episode's title calls a loophole is how New York's double jeopardy rule could shape a plea across both courts, as the show's write-up puts it. Episode 90 took Lindsay Clancy's 11-1 hung jury through the Massachusetts insanity standard and whether a retrial is possible. The show's newsroom has explained why prosecutors charged James Comey by information rather than indictment on the last day of what prosecutors say is a 10-year limit, reported a Washington grand jury's refusal to indict Cassidy Hutchinson, and previewed 24 gun petitions awaiting the Supreme Court's 6 November conference. Its Israel coverage runs from the October 7 border communities to the 27 October election.

That is the part a machine summary tends to lose. Researchers writing for Stanford's Institute for Human-Centered AI reported hallucination rates of 69% to 88% when they put specific legal queries to GPT 3.5, Llama 2 and PaLM 2, in a study published in January 2024. Those models have since been superseded. The problem has not gone away in news. A study of more than 3,000 answers from ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini and Perplexity, coordinated by the European Broadcasting Union and led by the BBC, found that "45% of all AI answers had at least one significant issue", VRT reported in October 2025. The difference between an information and an indictment, or between a dismissed federal count and an acquittal, is the kind of detail that decides a case and the kind a one-line summary flattens.

The show's answer is to put the lawyers' explanation where the search lands. YouTube said in February 2025 that it had more than 1 billion monthly active viewers of podcast content. By our count, 95 of the 98 entries in the show's catalogue carry chapter markers, 1,338 in all, a median of 14 an episode, many of them titled with the legal question. Episode 94's page publishes its 16 chapters as schema.org Clip markup. Google's video guidance says: "We will prioritize key moments set by you, either through structured data or the YouTube description." The Buzzsprout pages for episodes 84 and 94 carry full transcripts, Two Taps News lists 114 written stories that link back to episodes, and the catalogue lists 925 clips and Shorts.

Clips cut both ways. A 60-second cut can carry a lawyer's explanation to people who will never watch 48 minutes, and it can also strip out the caveat. The chapters and transcripts are the show's hedge: the clip points back to a long episode where the reasoning is on the record. "People search for a case, not for a podcast. When someone looks up a trial, we want a defense lawyer's explanation to be what they find," Rosenberg said. "A headline can tell you someone was charged. It can't tell you whether the charge was brought the right way, and that is often where a case is won or lost," he added.

Rosenberg, who runs a criminal defense practice in Fort Lauderdale, Florida, closes episodes with the creator's standard request: subscribe, because "it builds the channel, helps us more outreach, get more topics, get more guests". The show says nothing on it is legal advice. What it offers is narrower and, in a feed of summaries, rarer: lawyers who try cases reading the filings on camera, with the timestamps to find the part that matters. New full episodes usually arrive on Wednesdays, the show says, on its YouTube channel and the main podcast apps.
