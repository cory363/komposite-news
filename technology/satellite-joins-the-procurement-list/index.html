Satellite joins the procurement list

Low-orbit connectivity stopped being a last resort for remote sites and became a line item that carriers now resell alongside fibre.

Tom Kessler · Enterprise Technology Reporter
September 7, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Satellite connectivity used to arrive in enterprise networks as an exception: a site that fibre could not reach economically, procured separately, managed differently and mentioned apologetically. That has changed, and the change is commercial rather than technical.

The clearest marker is distribution. Industry reporting notes a major carrier adding low-orbit service to its managed enterprise portfolio, aimed at multi-site customers in places wireline cannot reach at sensible cost.

That matters more than any performance figure. A technology becomes a procurement option when it can be bought on the existing paper, with the existing service levels, from a supplier the buyer already has a contract with.

The performance is now adequate for ordinary corporate use rather than merely for emergencies, which removes the second objection. Latency from low orbit is high enough to notice in some applications and low enough to be irrelevant in most.

The applications driving adoption are unglamorous and specific. Multi-site retail, construction, offshore energy, mining, logistics and disaster recovery, all of which involve locations where the alternative is a leased line that takes a year and costs more.

The direct-to-device work sitting alongside it is a separate market with different economics. Connecting a handset or a sensor without a terminal is useful for coverage and messaging rather than throughput, and it is being sold as resilience.

The strategic question for a network team is the same one every new link type raises. A satellite path is a genuinely diverse route, and diversity only counts if the failover has been tested rather than assumed.

Cost has stopped being the differentiator and terms have started to be. Contract length, terminal ownership, and what happens to service quality as a cell fills up are where the negotiation now sits.

Capacity contention is the risk least discussed at the point of sale. Satellite capacity over a location is shared, and a site that performs well today is not guaranteed to perform well when the neighbourhood fills.

None of this displaces terrestrial networking, and it was never going to. What it does is remove the category of site that could not be connected at reasonable cost, which is a smaller claim and a genuinely useful one.
