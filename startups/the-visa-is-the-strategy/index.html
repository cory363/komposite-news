The visa is the strategy

There is no founder visa. What exists is a set of instruments used for purposes they were not designed for, and choosing among them shapes the company.

Grace Lindqvist · Startups & Venture Reporter
September 7, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

A founder who is not a citizen has to solve a problem the immigration system was not designed for. There is no founder visa. There is a set of categories built for other purposes, and a company gets shaped by which one fits.

Practitioners describe the routes plainly. Guidance for founders sets out a handful of options with different eligibility, timelines and trade-offs, none of which is a purpose-built path.

The extraordinary ability category has become the workhorse, because it has no annual cap and no lottery. It also requires assembling documentary evidence of achievement, which favours founders with publications, press and prior recognition.

That requirement quietly selects on background rather than promise. A second-time founder with coverage clears it comfortably; a first-time technical founder with a working product and no profile has a harder case to build.

The parole route for founders with qualifying investment exists and is used, and it carries a limitation that shapes planning. It grants time to build without granting a path to permanent residence, so the clock runs while the company grows.

The consequences show up in corporate decisions that look unrelated. Where to incorporate, whether to open an affiliated entity abroad, when to raise and from whom can all be driven by a status requirement rather than a commercial one.

Hiring is affected in the same way. A company that cannot sponsor cannot recruit from the pool of engineers already in the country on student or early-career status, which is where a large share of technical talent sits.

Counsel describe that pool as facing a measurably harder path to long-term status than a couple of years ago, which pushes the calculation toward hiring elsewhere or building distributed from the start.

Investors have adapted unevenly. Some funds retain immigration counsel and treat it as portfolio support; most treat it as the founder's problem, which is a strange position given how directly it determines whether the company can operate.

The practical guidance from people who have done it is to start earlier than feels necessary and to treat the route as a multi-year plan rather than a filing. It is not paperwork attached to the company. For a founder without status, it is a precondition of it.
