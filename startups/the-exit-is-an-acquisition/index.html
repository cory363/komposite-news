The exit is an acquisition

The listing is not the plan and has not been for years. Most companies that reach an exit reach it by being bought, usually for a modest sum.

Grace Lindqvist · Startups & Venture Reporter
September 4, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Founders raise money against a picture of a listing and exit through a purchase agreement. That gap between the story and the outcome is not new, but the proportions have become extreme enough that planning around the story is now a mistake.

The overwhelming majority of venture-backed exits are acquisitions. Cap table data covering this year describes an exit environment dominated by M&A, with the listing available to a small number of companies at the very top of the distribution.

Volume has held up better than the mood suggests. Reporting on deal counts describes a busy first half for startup acquisitions, with well-funded private companies among the most active buyers rather than the incumbents founders expect to be courting.

That last detail matters for how a company positions itself. The likely acquirer is increasingly another venture-backed company with a large balance sheet and a capability gap, which buys differently from a public strategic: faster, on product fit, with less patience for a process.

The size distribution is the part that founders find sobering. The median deal is modest and the large majority of transactions land below fifty million dollars, which is a good outcome for a company that raised a seed round and a poor one for a company that raised four.

That arithmetic is what determines whether an acquisition is a success. Preference stacks are unforgiving: a company sold for forty million after raising sixty returns capital to the last investors in and very little to anyone else, including the people who built it.

The acqui-hire sits at the bottom of the same distribution and is frequently described in the language of the top of it. A buyer taking the team and shelving the product is making a hiring decision with a transaction attached, and the press release rarely says so.

Artificial intelligence has changed the tempo more than the structure. Buying a team that has already solved a problem is faster than staffing one, and in a market where capability compounds monthly the difference between buying and building is measured in quarters that matter.

The practical implication for founders is to build the company so it is legible to a buyer well before anyone is talking about one. Clean cap table, transferable contracts, documented systems, and a product that can be described in a sentence to an engineering leader who was not looking for it.

Investors have adjusted expectations in a way that is not always communicated downward. A fund that underwrote a portfolio expecting a handful of listings now underwrites expecting a spread of acquisitions, and the target ownership and entry price follow from that.

None of this makes the outcome worse than it is. It makes the planning honest: the realistic destination is a buyer, the realistic price is smaller than the deck implies, and the companies that do best are the ones that were built to be bought without being built to be sold.
