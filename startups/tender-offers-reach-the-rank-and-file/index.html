Tender Offers Reach the Rank and File

Organized share buybacks were a founder's privilege in the last cycle. They have become a retention tool aimed at engineers.

Grace Lindqvist · Startups & Venture Reporter
August 26, 2026 · 2 min read

Equity in a private company has always carried an unstated condition: it is worth something when somebody buys the company or lists it. Companies staying private for a decade or more have turned that condition into a compensation problem, and the answer they have converged on is to run the liquidity event themselves.

The mechanism is a tender offer. The company, or a syndicate of its investors, offers to buy a defined slice of employees' vested shares at a set price during a set window. It is a private, scheduled, capped substitute for the market a listing would have provided.

The volume has moved sharply. Cap table data covering the first half of this year records tender activity at its highest level in years, with transaction value rising steeply against the same period in prior years.

What changed is not only the volume but the distribution. In the previous cycle, secondary sales concentrated at the top, with founders and early investors realizing gains while employees held paper. The current wave is aimed at engineering and operations staff, because that is where the retention problem is.

The reasoning is straightforward. An engineer four years into a vesting schedule at a company with no exit in sight is holding an asset they cannot spend, and a competitor offering cash compensation is making a concrete proposal against an abstract one.

The structure reflects that purpose. Eligibility is typically limited by tenure or level, and the amount an individual may sell is capped at a fraction of holdings, which lets someone buy a house without letting them exit the position entirely.

That cap is the whole design. A tender that allowed full liquidation would convert a retention tool into a departure subsidy, and the companies running these programs are explicit internally that partial is the point.

There is a cost to the practice that founders discuss less. A tender sets a price, and a price sets expectations. Running one at a valuation below the last primary round makes a paper reality concrete for the whole staff on the same afternoon.

Boards also inherit a cadence they cannot easily break. An annual tender becomes an expected part of compensation, and suspending it in a bad year sends a signal that no internal communication will soften.

For employees the arrival of a tender changes the calculation from whether the equity is worth anything to how much of it to convert. Advisers describe that as the harder question, because selling into a company-run window means selling without the information an investor in the same shares would have.

The broader effect is that staying private stopped requiring employees to fund the decision with their own patience. That was always the hidden term of the arrangement, and the companies that keep talent longest are the ones that stopped pretending otherwise.
