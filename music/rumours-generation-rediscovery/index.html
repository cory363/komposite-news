How Rumours keeps finding new generations

Fleetwood Mac's 1977 masterpiece has been rediscovered by every platform era, and its resilience explains how catalog culture works now.

Colin Abernathy · Culture Editor
June 2, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Rumours has been a hit in more eras than most artists get careers. Fleetwood Mac's 1977 album, written inside the band's own unraveling, returns to the charts with each new platform: radio made it, CDs re-sold it, and the streaming age keeps rediscovering it, most famously when a single skateboard video sent 'Dreams' back up the charts more than four decades after release.

Its durability is partly the songs and partly the story. The album's drama, bandmates writing about each other across the studio, gives every generation an entry point beyond the melodies, and the melodies would have been enough. It is the rare record that functions as both pop pleasure and mythology.

RelatedWhy Sticky Fingers still matters ›

For the modern catalog economy, Rumours is the proof case: a work old enough for grandparents that trends with teenagers, requiring no reunion, no remix, and no permission. The lesson the industry has drawn is now visible everywhere, in reissues, in sync strategy, in documentaries. The past is not competing with the present; on the platforms, it is the present.
