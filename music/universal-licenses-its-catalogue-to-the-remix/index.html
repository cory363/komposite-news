Universal stops litigating and starts licensing

A multi-year partnership with ElevenLabs would let listeners remix catalogue music, days after a rival AI company shipped a model trained on major-label material with permission.

Colin Abernathy · Culture Editor
September 11, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Two years of litigation between record labels and AI music companies was never really an argument about whether models would be trained on catalogue. It was an argument about whether training would be paid for, and the labels have now started answering their own question.

Universal Music Group has entered a multi-year partnership with the AI audio company ElevenLabs to build a platform that lets users remix songs. Gizmodo reported that the project is still in development and does not yet have a name.

The timing is the story. The announcement came days after Suno released its first model trained on major-label material with permission, leaving Universal as one of the few remaining holdouts among the majors.

Being the last holdout is a position with a short shelf life. Once competitors have licensed, the holdout is no longer establishing a principle about consent; it is declining revenue from a practice the industry has already accepted.

The product Universal describes is fan-facing rather than professional. It would give listeners ways to co-create with music from participating artists and songwriters, through remixes and mashups, new interpretations of tracks, and personalised vocal experiences.

The word participating is carrying weight in that sentence. It implies an opt-in at the artist and songwriter level rather than a blanket grant across the catalogue, which is the mechanism that determines whether this is a licence or an imposition.

The platform is to be kept separate from ElevenLabs' existing music products, including its Eleven Music generator. Keeping a licensed remix tool at arm's length from a general-purpose music generator is a legal boundary as much as a product one.

ElevenLabs' chief executive, Mati Staniszewski, said in a release that combining Universal's rights management expertise with the company's models would let artists and songwriters build new experiences for fans "and ensure they are fairly compensated."

Fair compensation is the term that will be tested, and it will not be tested in the press release. A remix platform generates an enormous number of low-value events, and the per-event economics of that are closer to user-generated content than to a stream.

The structural risk for artists is the same one streaming produced. A new format arrives, the rights are licensed at the corporate level, and the split is set by a contract signed before anyone knows what volume the format will reach.

What has genuinely changed is the position labels are arguing from. Suing a model trained without permission is a copyright case. Licensing the catalogue and selling the remix makes the label a participant in the format, and participants do not get to litigate it out of existence later.
