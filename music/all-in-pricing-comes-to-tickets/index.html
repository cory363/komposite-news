All-In Pricing Comes to Tickets

The fee was never the problem. Revealing it on the last screen was, and federal rules now require the number to appear first.

Colin Abernathy · Culture Editor
September 4, 2026 · 2 min read

The complaint about ticket fees was never really about their size. It was about when the buyer found out. A seat advertised at sixty dollars that costs eighty-five at checkout is a different product from one advertised at eighty-five, and the industry spent two decades defending the gap.

Federal regulators closed it. Analysis of the rule describes a requirement that live-event ticket sellers disclose the total price including mandatory charges, displayed more prominently than any other price on the page.

The mechanism being targeted has a name in the literature, which is drip pricing: revealing mandatory charges in stages so the buyer is committed by the time the total appears. It works because a purchase decision made at sixty dollars is hard to reverse at eighty-five.

The rule does not cap anything. A seller may charge whatever fee it likes, call it whatever it likes, and keep every dollar of it. The obligation is arithmetic rather than pricing: the first number a buyer sees has to be the number they pay.

Enforcement has followed. Reporting on the agency's actions describes a resale platform agreeing to refund ten million dollars to resolve allegations under the fees rule, which establishes that the requirement carries a price for ignoring it.

The competitive effect is the interesting part, and it cuts against intuition. Under drip pricing, the seller with the lowest advertised face value wins the click regardless of its total, which rewards moving revenue into fees. All-in pricing reverses that incentive in a single stroke.

Primary sellers and resale marketplaces experience the change differently. A marketplace listing thousands of sellers has to normalize fee structures it does not control, which is a harder engineering problem than it sounds and a harder commercial one.

Artists sit awkwardly in this. Fees are frequently the mechanism by which venue, promoter and platform are paid, and a transparent total makes visible how much of a ticket price never reaches the performer, which is a conversation the touring business has avoided.

States have moved in parallel rather than waiting, and several now impose their own transparent-pricing requirements on live events, which means a national seller faces a floor set federally and variations above it.

The predictable outcome is that advertised prices rose without anything actually costing more. A sixty-dollar ticket that was always eighty-five now says eighty-five, and the industry has spent the transition explaining that it did not raise prices.

That explanation is true and unpersuasive, which is roughly what an honest disclosure regime produces. The number was always the number. Buyers simply see it earlier now, and can compare two of them without arithmetic.
