Catalogs are a rates trade

A song's cash flows did not change when the discount rate did. What a buyer will pay for them changed a great deal.

Colin Abernathy · Culture Editor
August 19, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

The songs did not change. A recording that reliably earned a certain amount five years ago reliably earns something close to it now, and in many cases rather more. What changed is the rate at which a buyer discounts that stream, and it changed enough to reprice the entire market.

A catalogue is a long-duration income asset, which means it trades against a risk-free benchmark whether or not anyone involved describes it that way. When that benchmark sat near zero, a high multiple of annual earnings was defensible arithmetic rather than exuberance.

The peak reflected that. Multiples in the frothiest part of the last cycle reached into the twenties on a net publisher's share basis, and industry trackers now describe a market that has settled into a lower and steadier band without collapsing.

Settled is the operative word. The correction that followed the rate move was not a crash, because the underlying cash flows were largely real. Streaming income from a durable catalogue is among the more predictable revenue lines in entertainment, which is why the money arrived in the first place.

What has been squeezed hardest is the top of the valuation range. Analysts covering the sector describe an asset class that remains attractive against slower industry growth, which is a different proposition from the one sold when growth was accelerating.

Buyers have responded by getting more specific about what they are buying. A catalogue with forty years of consistent performance across formats behaves differently from a catalogue whose earnings history begins with one platform, and the market now prices that difference.

Genre has become part of the underwriting rather than an afterthought. Interest has broadened into repertoire whose listening base is growing rather than merely durable, on the reasoning that a catalogue's future is a demographic question as much as a musical one.

The seller's calculation shifted in the same motion. An artist deciding whether to sell is comparing a smaller lump sum against continuing to receive the income, and at a higher discount rate that comparison favors keeping it, which has thinned the supply of the very best catalogues.

That scarcity is why the market held rather than broke. Fewer premium assets came up, the buyers with capital were disciplined about price, and the clearing level found a band instead of a floor.

The structural change beneath all of this is that music became a recognized asset class with institutional owners, and institutional owners apply institutional arithmetic. The romance of owning a beloved catalogue is real and is not what sets the price.

Anyone reading catalogue headlines as a verdict on music is reading the wrong instrument. The number moved because the ten-year moved, and it will move again for the same reason.
