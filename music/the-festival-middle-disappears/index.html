The festival middle disappears

The largest events are more profitable than ever. Independent festivals are closing at a rate that has removed a fifth of them in three years.

Colin Abernathy · Culture Editor
September 7, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

A music festival is a business with one revenue event a year and twelve months of costs before it. That structure has always been unforgiving, and the conditions of the last few years have made it selectively fatal.

The attrition is concentrated rather than general. Trade reporting describes independent festivals closing at a far higher rate than corporate-backed events, with a substantial share of the independent sector gone since 2023.

Three costs moved at once and none of them is discretionary. Production and staffing rose sharply after the pandemic, headline artist fees rose as touring economics tightened, and insurance rose because the weather did.

Cancellation insurance is the least discussed and the most structurally important. It is what allows a promoter to commit to fixed costs against an outdoor event, and its price has risen with the frequency of weather-driven claims.

That produces a compounding problem specific to the independents. A promoter who cannot afford cancellation cover is a promoter carrying weather risk personally, and one bad weekend ends the business rather than the year.

Scale solves this in ways that have nothing to do with taste. A large operator runs a portfolio, absorbs a cancellation across it, negotiates artist fees against multiple dates, and buys insurance as a programme rather than a single policy.

The independents supply what the majors eventually book, which is where the loss stops being sentimental. Festivals are where an act learns to hold a field rather than a room, and that education happens at the scale that is disappearing.

Ticket prices have risen to meet the costs and have run into the obvious ceiling. An audience deciding between one festival and none is a different market from one choosing between several, and it books later, which worsens the cash flow that caused the problem.

Public support exists in places and is small relative to the arithmetic. A grant covers a shortfall; it does not change the insurance premium or the headliner's fee.

What is left is a market polarising into very large events that are extremely profitable and small ones run close to the edge, with the middle that used to connect them mostly gone.
