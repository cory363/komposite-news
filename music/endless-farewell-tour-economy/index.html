The farewell tour that never ends is a business model

Legacy acts have turned goodbye into a franchise, and the audiences keep coming because the songs are the point.

Colin Abernathy · Culture Editor
June 25, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

The farewell tour has become the least final event in music. Legacy acts announce the last run, extend it, rest, and return, and the audiences who bought tickets to say goodbye buy them again without complaint. The joke misses the economics: for catalog artists, the road is where the catalog becomes an event, and events are what the modern music business sells.

Live performance now carries the industry's premium revenue, and no one commands it like acts whose songbooks span generations. A stadium night built on fifty years of hits is multigenerational programming no new artist can match; grandparents, parents, and teenagers know different eras of the same set list.

There is a sunset built into the model, which is what gives it urgency and, oddly, its dignity. Every extension is priced against the knowledge that one of these tours really will be the last. The audiences are not being fooled twice; they are buying remaining chances, and both sides of the transaction know it.
