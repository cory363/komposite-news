Why Sticky Fingers still matters

Half a century on, the Rolling Stones' 1971 album remains the template for how a band turns danger into durability.

Colin Abernathy · Culture Editor
August 14, 20262 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Sticky Fingers arrived in 1971 with a new label, a new logo, and a band settling into the sound that would carry them for the next half century. It is the album where the Rolling Stones stopped being a sixties group that survived the decade and became an institution with its own iconography, the tongue, the swagger, the unhurried menace.

Listen now and what stands out is discipline underneath the danger. The country ballads sit beside the horn-driven rockers without strain; the playing is loose in feel and exact in construction. It is the record countless bands reach for when they want to sound effortless, and the reason so few manage it is that the effortlessness was built.

Its persistence is also a business story. The album marks the start of the Stones controlling their own catalog and image, decisions that made the band a lesson in longevity as much as in rock and roll. Every legacy act touring its classic album owes something to the blueprint drawn here: the songs endure, but so does the machine built to carry them.
