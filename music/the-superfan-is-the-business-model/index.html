The superfan is the business model

A small fraction of listeners generates most of the money an artist actually receives, and the industry has finally started building for them.

Colin Abernathy · Culture Editor
September 6, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Streaming pays for attention at scale, and most artists do not have scale. What they have is a small number of people who would buy almost anything they made, and the industry spent fifteen years building infrastructure that ignored them.

The arithmetic is not subtle. Commentary on the direct-to-fan shift describes an average independent artist drawing a small minority of income from the major streaming services, with the rest arriving from merchandise, physical sales, live shows and subscriptions.

That distribution makes reach the wrong metric for most working musicians. A hundred thousand casual listeners produce a number that will not cover a rehearsal room, and a few hundred committed ones produce a living.

Platforms have converged on the same shape in response: a paid tier, an artist-controlled feed, early access to releases, and a purchase path that does not route through a service taking the majority of the money.

The trade being made is legible once stated. Reach is exchanged for margin. A release given to a subscriber list reaches fewer people than one pushed into an algorithmic playlist, and returns considerably more per person who receives it.

The obligation that comes with it is the part artists underestimate. A subscription is a promise of continuing output, and a fan paying monthly for access has bought a relationship rather than a record.

That relationship does not scale in the way a catalogue does. Answering messages, recording the extra thing, and appearing regularly are labour, and they are labour performed by the person whose scarce resource is time to make music.

Choosing between the models turns on what the artist actually sells. Where the product is the record, a storefront works. Where the product is the ongoing relationship, a subscription works, and confusing the two produces a tier nobody renews.

The concentration also carries a risk that the enthusiasm around it tends to skip. A career funded by a few hundred people is a career with a few hundred points of failure, and no algorithm will replace them if they drift.

Still, the correction is a real one. An industry organised entirely around the casual listener was organised around the person contributing least, and building for the few who pay is not a retreat from the mainstream. It is where the money always was.
