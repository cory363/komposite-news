Vinyl is a real business again

Physical formats were supposed to be a nostalgia trade winding down. They are the fastest-growing line in recorded music.

Colin Abernathy · Culture Editor
September 6, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Physical music was supposed to be a nostalgia trade with a ceiling. The people who wanted a record had bought one, the argument went, and the line would flatten. It has not flattened. It is growing faster than the streaming business it was meant to be a footnote to.

Industry figures for the first half of this year record recorded music revenue up by a mid-single-digit percentage overall, with physical formats growing several times faster than that.

The composition is the surprise. Vinyl has grown steadily for years and continued to. The unexpected line is the compact disc, which was written off twice, once by downloads and once by streaming, and which is now growing faster in percentage terms than vinyl.

That is not a collector story. A CD is cheap to press, cheap to ship and cheap to buy, which makes it the format an artist can sell at a show and a listener can own without deciding to become the sort of person who owns a turntable.

The economics underneath explain why labels care about a small line item. A streaming play returns a fraction of a cent through a chain of intermediaries. A record sold directly returns several dollars, once, immediately, with no pool to be divided.

For an artist without a large streaming audience, that arithmetic is not marginal. A few hundred physical sales at a show can exceed a year of plays, which is why merchandise tables have quietly become a revenue strategy rather than a courtesy.

Manufacturing remains the constraint that nobody solved. Pressing capacity was dismantled through the format's decline and rebuilt slowly, so lead times are long and a release date is set partly by a plant's calendar rather than a marketing plan.

That lead time shapes what gets pressed. Independent artists order conservatively because unsold stock is a warehouse problem, and the runs that sell out do so partly because they were sized to.

Streaming remains the overwhelming majority of revenue and nothing here changes that. The physical line matters because it is growing, because its margin is better, and because it is the part of the business where the listener pays the artist rather than a platform.

The framing that has aged worst is the one that treated ownership as sentiment. People are buying objects because a subscription grants access to a library that can change, and an object cannot be removed from a shelf by a licensing dispute.
