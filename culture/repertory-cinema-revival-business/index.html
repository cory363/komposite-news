The repertory cinema is having its best decade in fifty years

Restored classics, anniversary runs, and 35mm programming are filling houses that first-run releases can't, and theater owners have noticed.

Colin Abernathy · Culture Editor
August 15, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

The healthiest screens in many cities are not showing new movies. Repertory programming, restorations, anniversary engagements, director retrospectives, midnight cults, has become the dependable draw of the theatrical business, filling houses on titles that finished their first run decades ago.

The economics explain the programming. A restored classic arrives with its audience already built, costs a fraction of a first-run booking, and turns a screening into an event that streaming cannot replicate at home. Younger audiences, raised with every film available and none of them urgent, treat a one-night 35mm engagement as the scarce thing worth leaving the house for.

Studios have learned to feed the circuit, mining their libraries for anniversaries worth polishing, while independent houses build identities around what they choose to revive. The result is a theatrical culture that looks, structurally, more like live music than like the multiplex era: the catalog is the canon, and the show is the event.
