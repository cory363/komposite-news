The window reopens, slowly

Studios spent years arguing that exclusivity was dead weight. They are now lengthening it, having found the downstream numbers disagreed.

Colin Abernathy · Culture Editor
August 16, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

For several years the theatrical window was described by the people who controlled it as an artifact of a previous business. Subscriber growth was the priority, exclusivity was friction, and the shortest possible gap between a cinema and a living room was treated as progress.

That position has reversed, and not quietly. At the industry's annual exhibition gathering, studios restated a commitment to windows of at least forty-five days, and one major distributor went further, committing to five weekends this year and seven from next.

The reversal was not driven by sentiment about cinemas. It was driven by results, and specifically by the discovery that the theatrical run was doing work that the release calendar had been treating as dead time.

Trade analysis of the shift describes longer windows becoming the prevailing orthodoxy after a period in which the opposite was assumed, with the strongest downstream performance attaching to films that had a real run rather than a token one.

The mechanism is marketing that a studio does not pay for twice. A theatrical release generates reviews, arguments, box office reporting and word of mouth, and a film that arrives on a streaming service carrying that history is discoverable in a way an unheralded title is not.

Compressing the window destroys that asset before it can be used. A film available at home within a fortnight never becomes an event, and the streaming release then has to manufacture attention from nothing, which is the most expensive marketing there is.

The window is also where a substantial part of a film's revenue is actually collected. Premium home rental at a real price captures buyers who would not have gone to a cinema, and that window only exists if theatrical exclusivity created the demand it monetizes.

What has not returned is the uniform ninety-day standard. The current arrangement is a floor with negotiation above it, and the length a given film receives depends on its performance, its distributor and what exhibitors extracted in the deal.

That variability is the genuinely new thing. Windowing has become a per-title decision rather than an industry convention, which means a strong opening can buy additional weeks and a weak one accelerates the move to home.

Exhibitors have gained less leverage from this than the headlines imply. The floor is real and the number of films receiving a wide theatrical release is smaller than it was, so a longer window on fewer titles is an improvement of uncertain size.

The underlying correction is simple enough. Exclusivity was not friction obstructing a modern distribution model. It was the thing that made a film worth watching later, and it took an expensive experiment to establish that.
