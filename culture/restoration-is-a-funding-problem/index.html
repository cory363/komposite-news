Restoration is a funding problem

The scanning technology is solved and affordable. What decides which films survive is a grant cycle and a shrinking number of specialists.

Colin Abernathy · Culture Editor
August 17, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Restoring a film is no longer a technical challenge in the way it was a generation ago. Scanners resolve more detail than the original negatives hold, and the software for repairing damage is mature. The constraint is money, and after money it is people.

The films most at risk are the ones least likely to attract either. A studio will fund the restoration of a title it can re-release, and that logic covers a thin band of cinema history. Everything outside it competes for grants.

Those grants are specific, small and oversubscribed. National preservation programs publish application windows and deadlines for basic preservation work, and the awards fund individual titles rather than collections, which tells you the scale relative to the need.

The underlying inventory has been surveyed. Research conducted for the national film preservation program produced a study of the state of American film preservation whose central finding was that a large share of the country's film history sits outside the studios that could pay to save it.

Chemistry sets the deadline. Nitrate stock decays on its own schedule regardless of storage discipline, and past a certain stage of deterioration a reel cannot be duplicated at all. The film is not endangered at that point; it is gone.

That makes triage the real work of an archive. Curators are deciding not which films deserve restoration but which ones will not survive long enough for a later decision, which is a different and much harder question.

The public-facing version of restoration obscures this. A celebrated release arrives with a comparison reel and a director's endorsement, and the impression is of an industry systematically working through its history. What it is working through is the commercially viable part.

Personnel are the constraint nobody budgets for. Handling degraded stock, assessing what a scan can recover, and making judgment calls about grain and color are learned over years, and the number of people who can do it competently is small and not growing quickly.

Digital output introduced a cost that never ends. A restoration produces files that must be stored redundantly and migrated onto new media indefinitely, which converts a one-time project into a permanent line item at institutions whose funding is annual.

Home video demand has quietly become part of the funding stack. Boutique labels licensing restored titles for physical release return money to the archives that did the work, which is a modest and genuinely reliable revenue line.

The reasonable summary is that restoration is a preservation question with a distribution byproduct, not the reverse. What is saved is largely determined by what somebody can pay for in a given grant cycle, and the chemistry does not wait for the next one.
