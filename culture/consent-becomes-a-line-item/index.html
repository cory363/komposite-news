Consent becomes a line item

Hollywood did not ban synthetic performers. It priced them, which turns a question about art into a schedule of permissions and payments.

Colin Abernathy · Culture Editor
September 4, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

The fear that organised the last few years of Hollywood labour disputes was replacement: that a performance could be generated rather than given. The settlement that emerged does not prohibit that. It requires permission for it, and payment.

Performers ratified a multi-year agreement covering television, theatrical and streaming work by an overwhelming margin. Coverage of the vote describes terms addressing artificial intelligence alongside a pension merger, carried with well over ninety percent support.

The union's own framing of its position is three words: consent, compensation, control. Read as policy rather than slogan, that is an acceptance that synthetic performance will exist, paired with a demand that it cannot happen to someone without their agreement.

Analysis of the bargaining cycle describes artificial intelligence remaining the central issue across the above-the-line unions, which is unusual: a technology question sitting at the centre of successive negotiations rather than appearing once and being resolved.

The practical consequence for a production is administrative. Using a digital replica now requires a documented grant covering the specific use, a payment attached to it, and a record that survives long enough to answer a question raised years later.

That paperwork is the actual protection. A right that exists without a record is unenforceable in a business where a performance can be licensed, resold and incorporated into something the performer never saw, and the record is what makes consent auditable rather than asserted.

It also converts an artistic anxiety into a budget conversation, which changes who decides. Whether to generate a crowd, a stunt, or an aged version of a face becomes a line the producer prices against the alternative, and it will be chosen when it is cheaper and rejected when it is not.

The gaps are where the leverage is unequal. A well-represented lead negotiates the terms of their own likeness; a background performer accepts what is offered, and the difference between consent and a condition of employment gets thin at the bottom of the call sheet.

Legislation is moving alongside the contracts rather than replacing them. Proposals to create a federal right over one's digital likeness would extend protection to people with no union to bargain for them, which is most of the people to whom this will eventually happen.

For the industry the honest reading is that a settlement has been reached rather than a principle established. Synthetic performance is permitted, documented and paid for, and the practice will expand to whatever the documentation allows.

That is what a negotiated outcome looks like when neither side can win outright. Nobody got a ban and nobody got a free hand, and the argument moves to the schedule of permissions, where it will stay.
