How a Vine Kid and a Netflix Editor Built a Monster for the App Age

RYDE strips the vampire myth down to a rideshare pickup, and the filmmakers behind it learned storytelling inside the same attention economy the film weaponizes.

Colin Abernathy · Culture Editor
August 23, 2026 · 3 min read

The résumés behind most indie horror films read a certain way. Film school, shorts, festival circuit, first feature. The résumés behind RYDE read like a history of the last decade of the internet, and that difference might be the most interesting thing about the movie.

Start with James Marchioni, the film's executive producer and one of its leads. He came up during the Vine years, working alongside some of the biggest creators on a platform that handed you six seconds to earn a viewer or lose them for good. That's not a side note in his bio. That's a formal education in attention, the kind no film program teaches, and RYDE is where he cashes it in, assembling a team around a single vision while stepping in front of the camera himself.

Then there's Paul Quattrocchi, who wrote, directed and edited the film. His background runs through work connected to MrBeast and editing for Netflix, which means he's operated at both poles of modern entertainment, the platform where retention is measured second by second and the one where craft still gets final say. On RYDE he holds every lever at once. The person who wrote the story is the person who directed the performances is the person deciding, frame by frame in the edit, exactly when you see what's coming and when you don't.

What did these two build with all that training? Something almost insultingly simple, which is how you know it's good. A young woman leaves a party after a fight, orders a ride, checks the plate, and gets in. The driver is not what he appears to be. That's the whole pitch. No lore dump, no prophecy, no ancient order of hunters. RYDE trusts the audience to bring the rest, because the audience already lives inside the scenario. Everyone reading this has stood on a curb at night matching three characters on a license plate to a phone screen, and nobody has ever counted it as the riskiest thing they did that week.

The old mythology is doing quiet work underneath. Vampires can't enter without an invitation. It's the rule that survived every era of the genre, from silent film through prestige television, and RYDE has noticed something sharp about it: the modern world turned the invitation into a button. She requests him. She confirms him. She opens the door herself. The monster's one great limitation, the thing that kept him waiting on the porch for a century of storytelling, now runs through an app, and the app never flags him.

Marchioni playing the driver sounds like ego on paper and reads as strategy in practice. The character works best as a blank, a polite professional you'd forget before the trip ended, and a producer who spent his formative years studying what makes audiences lean in knows exactly what to withhold. Quattrocchi's triple duty gives the film something rarer than a budget, a single point of view with no committee between the idea and the screen.

That's the real experiment here. Horror has always adapted to whatever its era fears, castles, suburbs, camcorders, group chats. RYDE bets that the sharpest fear available right now hides inside our most rehearsed convenience, and it was built by filmmakers who learned storytelling in the same ecosystem that built the convenience. The film's official site carries one line of marketing, and it lands like a terms-of-service clause nobody read: some rydes last forever.

The vampire used to need your permission. Now he just needs your request, and you've never once thought about who accepts it.
