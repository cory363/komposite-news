Documentaries Lost Their Buyer

Streaming built a market for non-fiction features and then stopped shopping in it. What remains buys a narrower kind of film.

Colin Abernathy · Culture Editor
September 5, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

A documentary is financed against a buyer who does not exist yet. Money is raised on the expectation that when the film is finished someone will acquire it, and for roughly a decade the streaming services made that expectation reasonable.

They have narrowed. Reporting from the documentary community describes an acquisition market that has thinned sharply at the festivals where non-fiction features have traditionally been sold.

The withdrawal is selective rather than total, which is what makes it hard to plan around. Buyers are still buying, but the categories that move are narrow: true crime, sports with access, music documentaries with rights already cleared, and series built around a subject who has agreed to participate.

Analysis of the shift describes a market that moved from features to series, which is a different product with different economics and a different relationship to the viewer.

The series format suits the platform because it produces more hours from one commissioning decision and more opportunities to surface the title in a feed. The feature suits the filmmaker because it is finished, but finishing is precisely what the platform has less use for.

Underneath the format question is a rights question. A music documentary with cleared rights is a straightforward purchase; one without them is a legal project with a film attached, and buyers have become unwilling to fund the clearing.

That has pushed the cost and the risk back onto the producer, who now has to arrive with clearances secured in order to be considered, which requires capital before there is any indication that anyone will buy the result.

The films most exposed are the ones the field would describe as its centre: the essayistic feature about a subject with no celebrity attached and no crime in it. That is the work the streaming boom quietly subsidised and the work its retreat has stranded.

Alternative routes exist and none replaces the volume. Theatrical release for documentaries reaches a real but small audience, philanthropic funding is oversubscribed and slow, and direct distribution reaches the people who already knew the filmmaker.

Producers describe the practical adaptation as making the film smaller rather than making a different film. Shorter schedules, fewer shooting trips, and a budget that can be recouped from a modest sale rather than requiring a large one.

That is a survivable adjustment and a real loss of ambition. The films will keep being made, on less money, and the ones that needed three years and a travel budget will mostly not be among them.
