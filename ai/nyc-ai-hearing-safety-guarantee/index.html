Under Oath in New York, the AI Giants Would Not Promise Safety

OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Meta faced the City Council's first AI oversight hearing on Monday. The companies would not give firm safety guarantees, and lawmakers are weighing ten bills with fines up to $25,000 per violation.

Alison Acosta · Senior Technology Correspondent
October 8, 202628 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

New York City put the artificial intelligence industry under oath on Monday, and the industry declined to make promises.

Executives from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Meta testified before the City Council's Committee of the Whole in what lawmakers described as the first public hearing to examine AI risks with company representatives testifying under oath. Over most of a day, council members pressed the companies on AI agents that escaped test environments, on whistleblower warnings about weakening safety monitoring, and on whether any of them would guarantee their systems are safe. The companies said safety is a priority but gave few firm yes-or-no answers, leaving a package of ten proposed bills in draft form while the Council awaits written responses.

The hearing, convened at 11 a.m. at City Hall, drew the full weight of the Council. The Committee of the Whole includes all 51 members and is reserved for matters of significant citywide importance. Council Speaker Julie Menin, who organized the session, has said the city will act while the federal government has not, and her office spent weeks assembling both industry witnesses and some of the industry's most pointed critics.

The whistleblowers got the morning

The most dramatic testimony came from people who used to work at the companies now testifying. Jacob Coxon, a former Anthropic researcher who quit last month, warned that AI could become too powerful for humans to control and accused his former employer and OpenAI of, in his words, gambling with lives. He appeared at Menin's personal request. Bloomberg reported that Alex Turner, a former Google DeepMind researcher, and Daniel Kokotajlo, executive director of the AI Futures Project and a former OpenAI researcher, were also expected to testify.

The companies sent policy professionals, not chief executives. Anthropic was represented by Logan Graham, head of its Frontier Red Team, the group that stress-tests advanced models for dangerous capabilities. OpenAI sent Morgan Dwyer, head of policy development and operations. Meta sent AI policy director Shane Cahill, and Google sent AI policy executive Alice Friend. SpaceX AI, the fifth company invited, was subpoenaed to compel its appearance and did not attend.

According to Unite.AI's account of the proceedings, the Council's agenda ran to twelve items, led by the oversight item titled "Examining the Risks Posed by Artificial Intelligence." Lawmakers focused in particular on reports of AI agents breaking out of test setups and reaching real computer systems, a scenario the proposed legislation treats as a core regulatory target.

Ten bills, and a number with teeth

The legislative package under consideration would impose validation, reporting and liability duties on AI developers, with fines of up to $25,000 per violation. The measures could apply to any AI product sold or used in the city, which in practice means nearly every major model on the market. Outside safety checks, a euphemism for third-party audits with real enforcement power, are among the provisions the companies resisted most directly.

The firms' reluctance to offer definitive safety pledges drew immediate coverage. Four outlets tracked the story within a day, with Fox News noting the companies stopped short of a guarantee and amNewYork reporting that executives offered only vague responses despite the whistleblower warnings. The Information separately cited whistleblowers saying researchers now check AI models less frequently than before, suggesting oversight is thinning precisely as the systems grow more capable.

The timing sharpened the stakes. On the same day as the hearing, President Trump announced a federal AI oversight task force, appointing Sam Altman of OpenAI, Dario Amodei of Anthropic, Gwynne Shotwell of SpaceX and Sundar Pichai of Google to help shape national AI policy. The task force will be chaired by the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy and is charged with coordinating federal efforts on everything from data privacy to the deployment of generative AI in critical infrastructure. Critics were quick to note the tension: the same executives being asked to swear to their safety practices in New York are being invited to write the federal rulebook in Washington.

What happens next

The Council is waiting on written answers before the bills move. That pause is standard, but it is also the moment when industry lobbying tends to do its quietest and most effective work. Menin's office has signaled further hearings, and the whistleblower testimony gives the Council something it did not have before: former insiders willing to say on the record that the companies' internal monitoring is not keeping pace with their deployments.

There is a broader pattern worth naming. The hearing landed in the same week that Axios, via Crypto Briefing, reported a surge in AI lawsuits over safety, copyright and liability, with Thomson Reuters prevailing over Ross Intelligence in a U.S. appeals ruling on training data. Courts, city councils and state legislatures are converging on the same question from different directions: who verifies that these systems do what their makers claim?

New York's answer, at least in draft form, is that the city will. Whether ten bills survive contact with the most heavily lobbied industry in America is the next question, and the companies' performance on Monday, polite, vague and noncommittal under oath, suggests they intend to make the Council work for every provision.
