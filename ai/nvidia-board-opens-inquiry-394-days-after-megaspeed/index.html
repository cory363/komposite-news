Nvidia's board opens an inquiry, 394 days after Megaspeed

Nvidia's independent directors are investigating reports that Jensen Huang was targeted by a foreign intelligence effort. Our timeline from company statements and filings shows the questions about its Singapore customer Megaspeed are more than a year old, and what the accounts stopped showing.

Dana Whitfield · House byline
November 9, 20267 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Nvidia's board said on Sunday that a committee of its independent directors, with outside counsel, will investigate reports that Jensen Huang, the company's founder and chief executive, "has been the target of a foreign intelligence effort". The statement names neither the directors nor the law firm, and says the board has confidence in Huang.

Its central sentence is a careful one: "While the Board has no reason to believe that either national security or NVIDIA secrets were compromised, the Board's inquiry will examine all available information." It does not mention Megaspeed, the Singapore-based cloud company at the centre of the reports, or Alice Huang, the Megaspeed executive whom US intelligence agencies suspect of passing information to Beijing, according to reports cited by CNBC.

Huang's own email to staff, posted on Nvidia's newsroom the same day, names neither of them either. "I was unfaithful to my wife," he wrote. "My actions hurt her and our children. There is no excuse for what I did." CNBC reported that the affair was with Alice Huang. On the security question, the email says only that the board has begun an investigation and that he will "cooperate fully". Nvidia told CNBC: "Jensen is committed to continuing to lead NVIDIA as CEO without interruption."

How long have the questions been public?

More than a year. By our count, 394 days passed between the first public confirmation of an official inquiry into Megaspeed and the board's statement. On 10 October 2025, Singapore's police told CNBC that "investigations are ongoing into Megaspeed for suspected breaches of our domestic laws", after reports that the company had helped Chinese firms get around US chip export controls. Nvidia said at the time that it had visited "multiple Megaspeed sites" and that Megaspeed was "running a small commercial cloud, like many other companies throughout the world, as allowed by U.S. export control rules", CNBC reported.

When the intelligence reports about Alice Huang appeared on 29 October this year, the company's line had not changed. "The diversion allegations against this regional cloud were thoroughly investigated and debunked last year," an Nvidia spokesperson told CNBC, adding: "We're confident that if the U.S. government believes that any person is a threat to American industry, it would let us know." Ten days later the board opened its inquiry. Megaspeed has said that inspections by the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security, Malaysia's trade ministry and Nvidia found "no evidence of any violations concerning the illicit transfer of chips or any illegal activities", CNBC reported on Monday. Singapore's government has said it did not investigate Alice Huang's alleged intelligence ties.

What do Nvidia's accounts show about Singapore?

Less than they used to. Until the autumn of 2025, Nvidia's financial statements broke out revenue by the country where customers were billed, and Singapore was the second-largest line after the United States. In the year to January 2025 it was $23.68bn, 18 per cent of revenue, according to the 10-K. In the quarter to July 2025 it was $10.16bn, or 22 per cent. Nvidia said repeatedly that this was an invoicing address, not a destination: "Customers use Singapore to centralize invoicing while our products are almost always shipped elsewhere." In its August 2025 quarterly report, it added that over 99 per cent of export-controlled data-centre compute revenue billed to Singapore was "for orders from U.S.-based customers".

Then the line went. In the quarterly report filed on 19 November 2025, 40 days after the Singapore police confirmed their inquiry, Nvidia began reporting revenue by the location of customers' headquarters instead. It explained the change in its own terms: billing locations "often reflected a customer's centralized invoicing location, even though our products were almost always shipped elsewhere", and it believed headquarters "provides a better representation of the geographic profile of our revenue". The filing does not mention Megaspeed, and we do not suggest the change was connected to it. Its effect is that a reader of the accounts can no longer see how much is invoiced through Singapore.

What changed when the old figures were restated?

Most of the Singapore revenue became American. Nvidia restates prior periods under the new method, so the same quarter can be read both ways. Comparing the July 2025 quarter as first reported with the version printed as a comparative a year later, US revenue rises by $9.43bn and Singapore's $10.16bn disappears, which fits the company's account of where its Singapore-billed customers were based. Revenue from customers headquartered in China, including Hong Kong, is also $1.22bn higher on the new basis than the China line on the old one, and "Other" is $0.86bn lower. Since the change, all customers outside the United States, Taiwan and China have accounted for between 1.3 and 2.0 per cent of revenue in each period reported, the filings show. Megaspeed is based in Singapore, so any revenue from it as a direct customer would now sit in that small line, which the filings do not break down further.

What happens next?

Two dates are already fixed. Nvidia reports third-quarter results on Tuesday 17 November, with a call at 2 p.m. Pacific time. The following day is the date that three Republican committee chairmen, Brian Mast of Foreign Affairs, John Moolenaar of the Select Committee on China and Rick Crawford of Intelligence, have asked Huang to brief their committees behind closed doors. Their letter of 5 November asked him to accept that date or propose another by Wednesday, and said: "It is vital that Congress determines the extent to which U.S. national security has been placed at risk." Nvidia has not said whether he will attend. Democrats have pressed separately. Senator Chris Coons, in line to chair the Senate Foreign Relations Committee from January, wrote that "Congress will investigate and get answers", and Representative Seth Magaziner asked for the commerce secretary, Howard Lutnick, to testify alongside Huang, CNBC reported.

As of 09:55 Eastern time on Monday, Nvidia had filed no Form 8-K about the inquiry with the Securities and Exchange Commission, by our check of EDGAR; its most recent filing was a director's routine report of a gift of shares. The board's statement promises "further updates as appropriate" and sets no timetable. A board inquiry run by independent directors reports to the board, and nothing in the statement commits it to publishing findings.

Why it matters: Nvidia's chips are central to US export policy toward China, and its chief executive has argued for broader chip exports there. The company's defence on Megaspeed has rested for a year on its own site visits and on export rules that allow remote cloud services. Its board is now examining the same relationship from a different angle, the security of the company's own information. The export-control system has been shifting from rule-writing to enforcement through penalties and settlements, and Nvidia's role in financing its own customers has drawn questions of its own, as when it considered guaranteeing debt for chips it sells. The results on 17 November will be the first chance for analysts to ask the company about any of it.
