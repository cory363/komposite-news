A hidden recorder, a Saudi royal and a bail fight

Federal prosecutors say the former OpenAI engineer charged over a recording device found in an executive conference room was courted by a member of the Saudi royal family and should stay in custody. His lawyers say he is no flight risk. None of the allegations has been tested in court.

Dana Whitfield · Senior AI Correspondent
October 19, 20264 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Federal prosecutors have asked a court to keep a former OpenAI engineer in custody, alleging that he was in contact with a member of the Saudi royal family and is a flight risk, the San Francisco Standard reported on Monday, citing a detention memo filed on Sunday. Abdulrahman Alfozan, 35, is charged with attempting to intercept oral communications over a recording device found in an OpenAI executive conference room. His lawyers dispute the government's picture of a man ready to leave the country. A magistrate judge was due to hear arguments on his release at 11:00 Pacific time on Monday; no ruling had been reported when this article was published.

The charge rests on events at OpenAI's San Francisco offices. According to the criminal complaint as summarised by The Register, staff found a small recording device on 30 September, wrapped in toilet paper and plugged into the USB port of a television in a conference room used for top-level meetings, including, officials said, non-public technical meetings and meetings with government representatives. Prosecutors allege that Alfozan, who joined OpenAI in July 2025, installed it on or around 28 September. He was arrested on 9 October and has been in custody since. The charge carries a maximum sentence of five years in prison and a $250,000 fine.

OpenAI could not recover any data from the device's memory card after making a forensic image of it, The Register reported, and officials have not said whether it captured anything. The company said it had "no indications of this individual inappropriately accessing or exfiltrating model weights" and that the employee had been fired. FBI Director Kash Patel said in a statement that the bureau treats the protection of American innovation as one of its highest counterintelligence priorities.

Sunday's memo goes further than the complaint. Prosecutors say a search of his home and a preliminary review of his phone turned up electronic components similar to those in the devices, and photographs dated 22 to 28 September that appear to show another concealed recorder in a second OpenAI conference room, according to the Standard. They also cite WhatsApp messages in which a member of the Saudi royal family, who is not named, urged him to join an AI initiative. In May, prosecutors say, the royal wrote "Saudi needs you more then the U.S.!"; Alfozan replied "100" and said he would not leave until his stock vested.

Prosecutors further allege that Alfozan applied in July to the Misk Foundation's 2030 Leaders programme, open only to Saudi nationals, and that between July and September he used an OpenAI productivity agent to ask about Saudi government engagements. They say his wife told FBI agents that she and their son were moving permanently to Saudi Arabia and that he planned to join them. The memo notes that the US has no extradition treaty with Saudi Arabia and says more serious charges could follow. In an earlier filing, on 8 October, prosecutors said he and his family had booked one-way tickets to Saudi Arabia via Istanbul, The National reported.

The defence motion, filed on Friday by his lawyers William Pernik and Nas Yasin, reads the same facts differently. It says Alfozan bought economy tickets to Riyadh on 13 September under his own name, 17 days before the device was found, for $1,897.59 for three travellers, with changes costing $180. It says he has a naturalisation interview on 6 November and a long record of foreign trips from which he always returned. The lawyers propose GPS monitoring, home detention in Millbrae and the surrender of his passports, with a bond signed by Bay Area supporters and backed by the Saudi Consulate General, which, the filing says, has written that it "would issue him no travel documents". They attached 54 letters of support. "That is not the profile of a person with one foot out the door," they wrote.

Pernik has also pointed out what is not in the case. The complaint does not charge Alfozan with stealing trade secrets or acting as an agent of a foreign government. "There is no evidence to suggest otherwise at this time that has been shared with us," he told The National last week, calling the allegations "wholly out of character for him". Monday's hearing concerns only whether he can be released before trial, not whether the government can prove any of its claims.

For AI developers, the case is a reminder that some of their most sensitive information still passes through ordinary rooms with ordinary televisions.
