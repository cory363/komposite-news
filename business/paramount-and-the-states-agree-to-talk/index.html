Paramount and the states agree to talk

A dozen attorneys general trying to stop a $111bn merger will sit down with the buyer at the end of October. California's attorney general says that is routine.

Jonathan Bright · Policy Editor
September 12, 20262 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

In merger litigation the calendar is a document. What the parties agree to do next, and when, is often the clearest available signal about what they think will happen.

Paramount Skydance and the coalition of a dozen state attorneys general seeking to block David Ellison's $111 billion merger with Warner Bros Discovery have set settlement talks for the end of October, with the Writers Guild of America also party to the discussions.

California's attorney general, Rob Bonta, played the development down as a standard step, which is both true and worth noting. Courts routinely require settlement conferences, and a date on a schedule is not a concession by anyone.

It is also the first time the principals have been required to sit in a room. In litigation of this size that is not nothing, because the cost of continuing rises steeply once discovery is properly underway and both sides can price it.

The composition of the opposition is the unusual part. A coalition of state attorneys general acting where federal enforcers have not is a pattern that has grown across technology and healthcare, and it produces a harder negotiation, because twelve offices have to agree on a remedy before one can be offered.

The presence of the Writers Guild changes the shape again. A union is not seeking the same outcome as a state enforcer: one is protecting a labour market, the other a consumer one, and a settlement acceptable to a regulator can be worthless to a guild.

What either could realistically extract is the question the talks will test. Structural remedies in media mergers have historically meant divesting a network or a studio; behavioural ones have meant commitments about licensing and output that are difficult to police for a decade.

The record on behavioural remedies is the reason the states are unlikely to accept one quickly. Commitments of that kind have repeatedly proved easier to promise than to monitor, and the monitoring burden falls on the offices least equipped to carry it.

For the buyer the incentive runs the other way. A deal of this size carries financing with a clock on it, and every month of litigation is a month of cost against an asset that is not yet generating the synergies the price assumed.

That asymmetry is what makes late October meaningful even if nothing is agreed there. The side with the deadline is the side that has to move, and the states know the shape of the buyer's calendar as well as the buyer does.

The likeliest outcome remains that the talks establish positions rather than settle anything. But positions established in October become the frame for a trial, and a coalition that has already agreed internally on what it would accept is a more dangerous opponent than one that has not.
