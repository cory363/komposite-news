Sourcing moves faster than tariffs

The rate schedule changes faster than a supply chain can be rebuilt, so companies have stopped optimising for any particular rate and started paying for optionality.

Priya Raghavan · Markets Reporter
September 4, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

A tariff schedule can change in a quarter. Qualifying a new supplier, validating its output and moving production takes years. Companies have spent this cycle discovering that the mismatch, rather than any particular rate, is the thing they actually have to manage.

The exposure is close to universal among manufacturers. Survey work on supply chain leaders reports the large majority describing a direct operational impact from trade policy changes, concentrated in imported raw materials and components.

The responses cluster into three moves, and they are not equivalent. Raising prices is immediate and reversible. Renegotiating supplier contracts is slower and partly reversible. Relocating production is slow, expensive and effectively permanent within any planning horizon that matters.

That asymmetry explains the sequencing. Companies reach for price first because it is the only lever that works on the timescale the policy moves on, and they reach for relocation last because committing to a location is a bet on a rate schedule that has already proven unstable.

Trade professionals now describe volatility itself, rather than the level of any tariff, as their leading concern. That is a meaningful distinction: a known high rate can be planned around and priced in, while an unknown one cannot be, and uncertainty is what stops capital being committed.

Inventory strategy has moved in the same direction. The shift from holding as little as possible toward holding a buffer is expensive in working capital and rational when the alternative is a line stoppage caused by a rule change nobody could have scheduled.

That is a real reversal of thirty years of practice. Just-in-time was built on the assumption that the cost of holding inventory exceeded the cost of disruption, and a period of frequent policy change has simply inverted the arithmetic.

Nearshoring gets described as the strategic answer and behaves more like a partial hedge. Moving assembly closer does not move the components, and a finished good assembled regionally from imported subassemblies carries most of the original exposure.

The businesses handling this best have stopped trying to predict. They qualify second sources they do not currently use, keep tooling in more than one place, and write contracts that allow volume to shift between suppliers without renegotiation.

That is optionality, and it is not free. It costs margin in every period where nothing happens, which makes it a difficult thing to defend in a quarter when the disruption did not arrive.

Which is the position finance teams are now in. The expenditure buys nothing visible when policy is stable and is the only thing that helps when it is not, and there is no version of the analysis that makes that trade-off comfortable.
