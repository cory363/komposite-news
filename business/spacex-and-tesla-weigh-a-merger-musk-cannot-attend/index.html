Two special committees, one conflicted chief executive

SpaceX and Tesla have each formed a special committee of independent directors to consider a combination, Elon Musk told SpaceX's earnings call on Tuesday. No terms are agreed, and on Tesla's side a deal would need a majority of all shares.

Tom Kessler · Enterprise Technology Reporter
October 21, 20263 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

SpaceX and Tesla have each formed a special committee of independent directors to consider combining the two companies, Elon Musk, chief executive of both, said on SpaceX's third-quarter earnings call on Tuesday, according to a transcript of the call. He said the committees have not agreed terms and that he is excluded from their talks.

"So both SpaceX and Tesla have formed special committees to discuss this idea at the request of shareholders," Musk said. "Because I'm a conflicted party, I can't actually be in the discussions of the special committees." He said "the special committees have not reached agreement on the terms of any transaction", adding: "there's no guarantee that they will." He said: "Do I think it would be a good idea, yes," but added that "it's really up to the special committees of both companies".

He also set out the bar. "Some people out there assume that a SpaceX and Tesla merger is a certainty," he said. On the Tesla side, "we will need a 50% vote of all shares, not just those who vote, but of all shareholders." Neither company has filed details of the committees. Tesla's proxy statement for its 3 December annual meeting, filed on Monday, does not mention them; it does disclose that Tesla's agreed $2bn investment in xAI became a right to SpaceX shares when SpaceX acquired xAI in February.

Musk sits on both sides at scale. His Schedule 13G on SpaceX, filed in August as of 30 June, reports beneficial ownership of 6.42 billion Class A shares, or 48.4 per cent, counting Class B shares held by his trusts, restricted Class B shares subject to performance conditions and 350 million options, all convertible into Class A. Class B shares carry ten votes each. Tesla's proxy gives him 699.6 million Tesla shares, or 19.8 per cent.

The results framed the call. SpaceX reported revenue of $14.6bn for the quarter to 30 September, up 161 per cent from $5.6bn a year earlier, and net income of $4.4bn against a net loss of $1.1bn, according to its earnings release. Diluted earnings were 35 cents a share against a loss of 36 cents. Income from operations was $2.8bn and adjusted EBITDA, a non-GAAP measure, $8.8bn. The company's numbers are its own.

The AI business drove the jump. Revenue in the Super Intelligence segment, which includes xAI's Grok models and the coding company Cursor, rose to $7.6bn from $813m, about 9.3 times by our arithmetic; Cursor, acquired on 14 August at an implied equity value of $60bn, contributed $608m. The segment accounted for $16.4bn of the quarter's $19.6bn capital spending, about 84 per cent by our arithmetic, as nameplate compute reached 1.7 gigawatts. Starlink's Connectivity segment brought in $5.2bn, up 69 per cent, with 13.6 million consumer subscribers paying an average $69 a month.

The guidance is steeper still. Bret Johnsen, the chief financial officer, said SpaceX expects fourth-quarter capital expenditure of "$35 billion to $40 billion", roughly 1.8 to 2 times the third quarter's by our arithmetic, and "total company revenues in the range of $33 billion to $37 billion for Q1 of 2027", a midpoint about 2.4 times the quarter just reported. He put the backlog at $63bn at 30 September, about $42bn of it expected to be recognised within a year.

Why it matters: with Musk on both sides, the decision rests with independent directors at each company, and Tesla's threshold of a majority of all shares outstanding, rather than of votes cast, is the figure to watch. Neither company has given a timetable. Komposite has followed SpaceX's spending, including its $40bn chip financing and its 800 MHz spectrum purchase.
