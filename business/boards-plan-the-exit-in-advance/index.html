Boards plan the exit in advance

Chief executive departures ran at records for two years. The change worth noticing is that a growing share of them were arranged rather than forced.

Priya Raghavan · Markets Reporter
September 7, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Two consecutive record years of chief executive departures reads as instability. The more interesting number underneath it is how many of those departures were arranged in advance, which has risen sharply.

Tracking of global turnover describes a marked increase in the share of exits occurring through planned succession, alongside a first half this year that ran below the same period last year.

That combination suggests something other than turmoil. Boards appear to be treating succession as a scheduled governance function rather than an emergency response, which is what the literature has recommended for decades and what boards have mostly not done.

The difference matters operationally rather than symbolically. A planned handover has a named successor, an overlap period and a strategy that survives the transition. A forced exit has an interim, a search and a year of deferred decisions.

Pressure from activist investors is part of what produced the discipline, which is an uncomfortable observation for boards that resisted it. Campaigns rose sharply, and a board that expects to be asked about succession prepares an answer.

The other shift is who gets hired. A growing share of incoming chief executives have run a public company before, which is a preference for demonstrated experience over internal development.

That preference has a cost the hiring data does not show. A company that reaches outside for a proven operator is a company whose internal pipeline did not produce a candidate, and each such decision makes the next one more likely.

Sector variation is wide and tells its own story. Consumer businesses saw the highest turnover, which is what happens when demand softens and the chief executive is the most legible thing a board can change.

For investors the useful signal is not the departure but its character. An announced transition with a named successor and a defined date is a governance event; an immediate exit with an interim appointment is a warning about something not yet disclosed.

The stabilisation this year is too recent to call a trend. What has plausibly changed is that boards now treat the question as one they should be able to answer at any moment, rather than one they will address when it arises.
