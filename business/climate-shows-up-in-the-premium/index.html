Risk

Climate shows up in the premium

The insurance market prices physical risk before any regulator requires it to be disclosed, and the bill arrives annually.

Priya Raghavan · Markets Reporter
September 7, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Corporate climate disclosure has been argued about for a decade. While that argument continues, one market has been pricing physical risk annually, without waiting for a standard, and sending the result as an invoice.

The repricing is regional rather than general. Analysis of commercial property cover describes physical hazard repricing concentrated in coastal, wildfire and flood-exposed areas, with increases in those zones far above the market average.

That concentration is what makes the aggregate figures misleading. A national average that looks moderate contains portfolios seeing very little movement and specific assets seeing increases large enough to change whether they are viable.

Reinsurance is the transmission mechanism and it is rarely visible to the buyer. When reinsurers raise prices or narrow capacity, primary carriers either pass it on or retain less risk, and both routes end at the same policyholder.

The consequences run past insurance into financing. Lenders require cover, so an asset that cannot be insured economically is an asset that cannot be borrowed against, which is a valuation event arriving through an underwriting decision.

That link is what makes this a corporate finance question rather than a facilities one. A property portfolio's insurability is now part of its cost of capital, and it is being reassessed every renewal.

Deductibles have moved alongside premiums and get less attention. A policy with the same headline price and a much higher retention has transferred risk back to the buyer without appearing as an increase.

Companies responding well have started treating renewal as an engineering exercise. Documented mitigation, hardened assets and loss history presented properly change the price, because underwriters are pricing uncertainty as much as hazard.

Which points at the useful reframing. The insurer is not asking whether a company believes in climate risk. It is asking what happens to this specific building in a specific event, and pricing the gap between what is known and what is asserted.

The disclosure rules will eventually arrive and will largely formalise information the insurance market has already extracted. The premium got there first, and it is the version with a due date.
