Microsoft offers buyouts to about 60,000 staff

Microsoft has opened a Voluntary Exit Offer to about 60,000 employees on participating teams in the US, Canada, Britain and India, the company confirmed on Monday. By our arithmetic that is about 27 per cent of its full-time workforce.

Tom Kessler · House byline
October 26, 20263 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Microsoft is offering voluntary buyouts to about 60,000 employees, roughly 27 per cent of its full-time workforce by our arithmetic, the company confirmed to TechCrunch on Monday. The programme, called the Voluntary Exit Offer, was announced to staff by Amy Coleman, Microsoft's executive vice president and chief people officer, in a memo the company published on its official blog. Eligible employees were told they would receive an email with the details of their offer within hours.

The memo sets out the scope but not the size. It says the offer is available to eligible employees "in participating teams in Canada, India, the United Kingdom and the United States at level 70 and below", and describes it as "a time-limited, voluntary program" offering "financial support and career planning resources". It adds: "Some organizations are providing the Voluntary Exit Offer, and some are not." Participation, it says, varies with each business's priorities, customer commitments and workforce needs. The memo gives no headcount, deadline or payment terms; the 60,000 figure comes from Microsoft's confirmation to reporters.

Ms Coleman wrote that the offer "gives eligible employees who may be considering a future outside the company a new choice to make that transition now with support", and that it "also gives participating organizations another way to adapt their workforce to changing needs". She tied it to a retirement offer made in the spring: "This builds on what we learned earlier this year through the Voluntary Retirement Program." And she was candid about its novelty: "The Voluntary Exit Offer is new for us. We'll learn from it."

Some details reported on Monday go beyond the memo. TechCrunch and KIRO 7 in Seattle described eligibility as reaching the vice-president level and below; the memo itself says "level 70 and below", and Microsoft does not publish how its internal levels map to titles. Bloomberg reported, according to TechCrunch, that teams including Xbox, Windows, Copilot and Finance are taking part, and that some employees, including those in data centres and in sales and marketing, are excluded. TechCrunch said the terms would be similar to Microsoft's standard severance. Those details were not independently verified.

Microsoft's own filing gives the denominator. Its annual report for the year to 30 June says it employed about 223,000 people full-time, split between functions as follows.

By our arithmetic, 60,000 is 26.9 per cent of that total. If sales and marketing staff are excluded, as Bloomberg reported, the remaining pool is 180,000, and the offer reaches one in three of them. The 10-K counts data-centre staff inside its 89,000 operations figure, so they cannot be removed from the calculation separately. The headcount is four months old, and an offer is not a departure; the memo gives no expected take-up.

The spring programme was much narrower. In April Microsoft offered voluntary retirement to about 8,750 US employees whose age and years of service added up to 70 or more, about 7 per cent of its US staff, TechCrunch reported at the time, citing CNBC and Bloomberg. More than 30 per cent of those eligible accepted, according to Bloomberg as relayed by TechCrunch, which by our arithmetic is more than 2,600 people. Monday's offer is open to about 6.9 times as many employees, across four countries rather than one.

Why it matters: a voluntary offer lets a company shrink teams without the disruption of compulsory layoffs, and Microsoft is making it at a time when investors are asking where the returns from AI spending show up. Komposite reported on Sunday that Apollo's chief economist found the AI payoff concentrated in seven companies, of which Microsoft is one. In September Komposite reported that Microsoft's new Copilot app splits its pricing between per-user licences and metered credits. How many of the 60,000 take the offer will be the measure of whether it changes the workforce or only tests it.
