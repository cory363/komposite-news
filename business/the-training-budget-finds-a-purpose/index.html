The training budget finds a purpose

Corporate learning spend was a compliance function with a course catalogue. It is being redirected at a skills gap most employers say they have.

Priya Raghavan · Markets Reporter
September 7, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Corporate training has spent two decades as a function nobody measured. Budgets were defended on completion rates, courses were assigned rather than chosen, and the connection between spending and capability was asserted rather than demonstrated.

That is changing because the gap became specific. Industry figures put corporate training investment above a hundred billion dollars and growing, with digital and AI readiness the stated priority for the coming year.

A specific gap is easier to fund than a general one. "Our people need development" competes badly for budget; "most of our workforce cannot use the tool we just deployed to all of them" does not.

The reported gap is large and self-assessed, which is worth holding lightly. A substantial majority of companies say they lack the relevant skills internally, and self-reported skills gaps have historically tracked anxiety as closely as capability.

Where the money goes is more informative than how much of it there is. Spending is concentrating on a narrow band of practical capability rather than broad literacy, because the measurable outcome is whether a deployed tool gets used.

That narrowness is a strength for the budget and a weakness for the workforce. Training someone to operate a particular system raises this quarter's utilisation and does little for their position if the system is replaced.

The largest programmes have come from the companies selling the technology, which is a genuine contribution and not a neutral one. A vendor-funded credential is training and marketing at once, and the curriculum reflects both purposes.

The distributional question is the one employers answer least well. Spending clusters on employees already close to the technology, and the workers whose roles are most exposed to automation are frequently not the ones being retrained.

That is a rational allocation for a company and a poor one for a labour market, and no employer is positioned to fix it alone.

The genuine change is that the budget now has a thesis. Whether it produces durable capability or a large number of certificates will not be visible for several years, which is roughly how long these programmes have to run before anyone can tell.
