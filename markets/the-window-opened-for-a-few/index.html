The window opened for a few

Listing volumes look like a full recovery. A handful of enormous names carry the number, and the queue behind them has not moved.

Priya Raghavan · Markets Reporter
September 6, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Listing volumes this year read as a decisive reopening. They are better understood as a small number of very large transactions, which is a different market from the one most private companies are waiting for.

The totals are genuinely large. Tracking of the pipeline describes prospective technology listings valued in the trillions, with artificial intelligence and adjacent companies accounting for the overwhelming majority of that value.

Concentration of that kind changes what the aggregate means. A market where a few names raise most of the capital has told you about those names, and almost nothing about the hundred companies behind them wondering whether to file.

For the companies in that queue, the relevant question is not whether the window is open but whether it is open at their size. A billion-dollar listing and a three-hundred-million-dollar listing are different products sold to different buyers.

The smaller end has structural problems that a good year does not fix. Index inclusion thresholds, analyst coverage economics and the minimum float institutional buyers require all favour scale, and none of them moved.

That is why the private alternatives kept growing through the reopening. Tender offers, continuation vehicles and secondary sales exist because they solve the liquidity problem without requiring a company to be large enough for the public market to want it.

Concentration by sector compounds the concentration by size. A pipeline weighted this heavily toward one theme is exposed to sentiment about that theme, and a repricing there would close the window for companies with no connection to it.

Bankers describe the practical filter as narrower than the headline suggests: durable revenue growth, a credible path to profitability, and a story that a generalist can hold. The middle of the market has always struggled with the third.

The counterargument is that reopening is sequential rather than simultaneous, and that large successful listings create the conditions for smaller ones by demonstrating that the buyers are there.

That has been true in previous cycles and it takes longer than founders expect. The queue clears from the top, slowly, and a company that has waited three years is being asked to wait through the demonstration.
