Margin debt slips 3.6 per cent from its June record

Investors owed their brokers $1.448tn at the end of October, FINRA figures show, down from $1.502tn in June. The run-up into June was the steepest before a peak since 2007, by our analysis of the series since 1997.

Priya Raghavan · House byline
November 17, 20263 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Investors owed their brokers $1.448tn in margin debt at the end of October 2026, 3.6 per cent less than the record $1.502tn of June, according to monthly figures that the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority posted on Friday.

The FINRA statistics count debit balances in customers' securities margin accounts at member brokers, money borrowed against shares and other securities. October's figure was down 2.1 per cent from September's $1.480tn and still 22.4 per cent higher than a year earlier, by our arithmetic. Free credit balances, the cash customers keep at brokers, were $210.3bn in cash accounts and $212.9bn in margin accounts. Netting both off the debit balance leaves $1.025tn of borrowing, up 29.7 per cent on October 2025.

Most of the rise came in three months. Debit balances grew 6.8 per cent in April, 8.5 per cent in May and 6.1 per cent in June, adding $281.2bn between March's $1.221tn and the June record, by our arithmetic. Margin debt had first passed its previous high, October 2021's $935.9bn, in January 2025. Free credit balances have grown far more slowly: up 6.2 per cent on the year in cash accounts and 9.0 per cent in margin accounts.

How unusual was the climb into June?

Steeper than before any peak in the series except those of 2000 and 2007. Komposite took FINRA's spreadsheet, which runs monthly from January 1997, and marked each month that was the highest within 18 months on either side. There are seven. Margin debt rose 49.0 per cent in the 12 months to June 2026, from $1.008tn in June 2025. The rise into March 2000 was 80.5 per cent and into July 2007 62.6 per cent. The rise into October 2021, the last peak before this one, was 41.9 per cent.

After every earlier peak, debit balances fell at least 13.8 per cent within a year. After the 2000 and 2021 peaks they fell by more than 30 per cent. So far the drop from June has been smaller: the low was July's $1.417tn, 5.6 per cent below the record, and the figure climbed again in August and September before October's decline. Those six episodes are a small sample, and a peak can only be identified with hindsight; the table shows what followed, not what will follow.

Prices have not fallen much. The S&P 500 closed at 7,499.36 on 30 June and 7,580.07 on 30 October, a rise of 1.1 per cent, according to FRED, while margin debt fell 3.6 per cent over the same months. The index then lost 2.2 per cent to 7,416.59 by Monday's close. FINRA's November figure, which will show whether borrowing kept falling as prices did, is not due until next month.

What the figures do not show

FINRA's series covers member broker-dealers only. It does not include loans that banks make against securities, leverage inside funds, or the borrowing embedded in options and leveraged exchange-traded products. Debit balances also tend to rise with prices, because higher collateral values let investors borrow more against the same holdings. A drop of 3.6 per cent from the top is modest next to the falls in the table.

Why it matters: margin loans are callable, and when prices fall, brokers can demand more collateral or sell positions, which can add to the decline. The rise into June was large by the standards of the past 30 years, and the unwinding has been slow. Komposite has reported on other pockets of leverage this year, including the hedge-fund basis trade in Treasuries, and on bitcoin's November slide, during which the largest bitcoin fund shed 46m shares.
