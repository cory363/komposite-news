Treasury Yields Hit Multidecade Highs and Stocks Buckled

The 10-year touched 5.36 percent, its highest in 24 years, as a weak jobs report collided with stubborn inflation fears.

Elena Vasquez · Markets Correspondent
October 7, 20263 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

U.S. stocks fell as volatility in Treasury yields sparked concerns about mortgage rates and corporate credit costs, the Wall Street Journal reported.

The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 341.41 points, or 0.66 percent, to 51,179.87. The S&P 500 lost 17.16 points, or 0.22 percent, to 7,801.77, down from a record high. The tech-heavy Nasdaq Composite slipped 61.20 points, or 0.22 percent, to 27,538.69. The Russell 2000 index of small-cap stocks fell 1.3 percent to 2,793.20, roughly 9 percent below record highs from earlier this year and on the cusp of correction territory.

Stocks were down sharply in early trading after a spike in Treasury yields intensified, then pared losses when yields eased in the wake of a strong auction. The yield on the 10-year Treasury note hit a 24-year high of 5.361 percent before settling at 5.276 percent. The yield on the 30-year bond rose to 5.660 percent, on the verge of its highest level since 2002.

The bond market's message is that investors are demanding a hefty premium to hold long-dated U.S. debt. Heavy government borrowing and energy costs are keeping that premium sticky, and when the risk-free rate is that high, every share price has to work harder to justify itself.

The Fed's mixed signals

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh has deliberately played down the importance of forward guidance, seeking to refocus investors on economic data. But markets keep hanging on policymakers' every word. At the end of September, the probability of an October rate hike briefly surged to 70 percent, then fell to around 25 percent after New York Fed President John Williams and Vice Chair Philip Jefferson both indicated there was no need to act urgently, according to market analysis.

Friday's soft jobs report did the Fed a favor on that front. Nonfarm payrolls grew by just 29,000 in September against expectations of roughly 90,000, with the jobless rate up to 4.2 percent. Traders now largely expect the Fed to hold rates this month, though a December rise is still priced in.

The mortgage squeeze

The bond moves are causing real pain in the mortgage market. The average fixed 30-year mortgage rate in the U.S. was 7.52 percent, hovering around three-year highs, according to Bankrate. The SPDR S&P Homebuilders exchange-traded fund declined 2.6 percent and is now down more than 8 percent year to date.

Strategists at Bank of America Global Research described the worldwide bond bear market of 2026 as largely a response to changing central-bank policy. Minutes from the Fed's September meeting, released Wednesday, showed most officials anticipated another rate hike this year, with some casting the plan as insurance against future inflation rather than a response to current conditions.

The selloff is all the more striking because it interrupted a record run. The S&P 500 closed above 7,800 for the first time just days earlier, and the Nasdaq set fresh records on the back of the soft jobs report. But concentration remains extreme: more than 30 percent of the S&P 500's market capitalization sits in the Magnificent Seven AI-linked tech stocks, leaving the index unusually sensitive to any repricing of growth expectations.

Energy is adding to the uncertainty. Intensified attacks near the Strait of Hormuz have raised shipping, freight and insurance costs, injecting an energy-driven inflation risk just as the Fed weighs whether price pressures are truly contained. For bond investors, it is one more reason to demand compensation for holding long-dated paper.

For now, the market sits in an uneasy equilibrium. Stocks remain near records, but every new high in yields raises the bar for the earnings needed to sustain them. Upcoming inflation data will offer clearer signals about the Fed's next move, and traders will be watching.
