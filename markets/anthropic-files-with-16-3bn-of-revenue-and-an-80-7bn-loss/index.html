Anthropic files with $16.3bn of revenue and an $80.7bn loss

Anthropic filed publicly on Monday to list on Nasdaq under the ticker ANT. Its prospectus shows first-half revenue of $16.3bn and a net loss of $80.7bn, of which $72.4bn came from revaluing convertible notes as its valuation rose.

Priya Raghavan · House byline
October 26, 20266 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Anthropic, the developer of the Claude AI models, reported a net loss of $80.7bn on revenue of $16.3bn for the first six months of 2026, according to the registration statement it filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission on Monday. Of that loss, $72.4bn came from a single line: the change in the fair value of its convertible notes and related instruments, which the company attributes to the rise in its own valuation. The filing is the first public set of audited annual and unaudited half-year accounts for the company.

Anthropic has applied to list its Class A common stock on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the symbol "ANT", the prospectus says, and the company said in a statement that the number of shares and the price range had not been set. The prospectus leaves those figures blank, as it does the proceeds and the size of the underwriters' option. Some shares will be sold by existing stockholders, from whom the company will receive nothing. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley head a cover page that lists 22 banks by our count, including J.P. Morgan, Citigroup, Barclays and Wells Fargo. The filing gives no date for the offering.

The fair-value line is the largest item in the accounts. Headed "change in fair value of convertible notes, derivative liabilities, and SAFEs", it cost $72.4bn in the half, a movement the filing says was "primarily driven by an increase in the fair value of our outstanding convertible promissory notes resulting from an increase in our enterprise valuation". A further $8.8bn of other expense arose mainly from fair-value accounting on assets and liabilities under note purchase agreements, the filing says. These are remeasurements rather than cash payments: the notes outstanding at 30 June were all held by Amazon and are to convert into non-voting Class N shares immediately before the offering. The higher the company's valuation, the more those shares are worth, and the larger the charge.

Below that line, the operating business lost less. The operating loss narrowed to $693m in the first half from $2.1bn a year earlier, an operating margin of minus 4 per cent, while $1.1bn of interest income came in on the company's cash. Anthropic also reports its own non-GAAP measures. Adjusted operating income, which excludes $2.2bn of stock-based compensation and a $126m non-cash charge for an employee donation-matching programme, was $1.6bn, a 10 per cent margin. Adjusted net income, which also excludes the fair-value charges, was $2.7bn. Those measures are the company's own, and the half-year accounts are unaudited.

Revenue rose 1,300 per cent from $1.2bn in the first half of 2025, the filing says. Consumption-based revenue, mainly usage of its models, accounted for $12.1bn of the increase and subscriptions for $3.0bn. Full-year revenue was $41m in 2023, $386m in 2024 and $4.6bn in 2025. Customers paying at least $100,000 a year rose from more than 600 to more than 6,000 between June 2025 and June 2026 and accounted for about 74 per cent of first-half revenue. By 30 September the company counted more than 11,900 such customers, more than 1,900 paying at least $1m and about 250 paying at least $10m.

Compute is the largest cost. Compute and infrastructure expenses rose 283 per cent to $8.9bn in the half but fell to 55 per cent of revenue from 201 per cent a year earlier, because, the filing says, revenue grew "against a compute base that was primarily committed in advance and did not fluctuate with in-period demand". Sales, marketing and partnerships spending rose to $3.2bn, driven by a $2.7bn increase in fees paid to channel partners. The company has also signed purchase commitments and leases, substantially all for compute and infrastructure, running to 2045; the total is left blank in this version of the prospectus.

Anthropic said it had raised $141.1bn of capital in aggregate by 30 June and held $112.4bn of cash and cash equivalents and $19.3bn of short-term investments, or $131.7bn combined by our addition, with a further $3.2bn available under a credit facility. Earlier years show the same pattern in the accounts: of the $42.0bn net loss in 2025, $34.3bn came from the fair-value change on the same instruments. The 2025 accounts also carry a $1.5bn charge for the settlement of Bartz, a copyright class action, which the filing says a district court approved in July 2026.

Public shareholders will buy economic rights with limited control. Anthropic is a public benefit corporation, required to balance shareholder returns with its stated purpose of "the responsible development and maintenance of advanced AI for the long-term benefit of humanity". Its Long-Term Benefit Trust, described in the filing as a body of "financially disinterested trustees", will hold Class T shares that let it elect directors who make up a majority of the board. A single Class F share, held by a company owned by the seven co-founders, including chief executive Dario Amodei and president Daniela Amodei, will carry 50.1 per cent of votes on other matters. Neither class has economic rights. Employees will receive Class E shares with limited votes, and strategic partners hold non-voting Class N stock. The company says it will be a "controlled company" under Nasdaq rules.

Two of those partners will be large holders. After its preferred stock and convertible notes convert, Amazon and Google will each hold more than 5 per cent of Anthropic's capital stock, the filing says. Amazon's notes are capped so that no portion converts if Amazon would then hold more than 12 per cent of the company's capitalisation on the filing's measure, unless Amazon waives the limit.

The risk factors run from page 26 to page 119. Among them, the company says concerns have been raised "both within our organization and by other researchers, regulators, policymakers, and industry participants" that sufficiently advanced AI systems could, without appropriate safeguards, "pose catastrophic or existential risks to humanity". It adds that it has observed that its models "are contributing to the pace of our own research".

Traders reacted before the shares exist. On Hyperliquid, a crypto derivatives venue that lists a contract tracking Anthropic's expected share price, the "ANTH" contract rose to about $1,980 around 09:00 EDT, when the filing was due, before falling about 6 per cent to roughly $1,857 within minutes, CoinDesk reported. The contract is a derivative, not a share, and its price is not a valuation set by the company or its underwriters.

Why it matters: this is the first full public set of accounts from one of the frontier AI developers, and it lets investors check claims about AI economics against a regulatory filing rather than press reports. The filing shows revenue growing faster than compute costs and an operating loss that has narrowed, alongside compute commitments running to 2045 whose total is not yet disclosed, a net loss dominated by note revaluations and a share structure that gives public holders a limited vote. Komposite reported last week that OpenAI was seeking about $30bn before Anthropic's listing, and earlier that Anthropic plans to commit 5 per cent of its shares to a public benefit fund, subject to stockholder approval.
