A second Meta share, without the vote

Meta will give shareholders one non-voting Class C share for every share they own from January, so it can raise money without diluting Mark Zuckerberg's 60.6 per cent of the votes. An agreement ties most of his new shares to his voting stock.

Priya Raghavan · Markets Reporter
October 22, 20265 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Meta Platforms will give shareholders one non-voting Class C share for every Class A or Class B share they hold once its new charter takes effect on 19 January 2027, an 8-K filing shows. The company said the stock can be used to raise capital, pay employees and fund acquisitions without diluting anyone's vote.

The board approved the restated charter on Monday 19 October on the unanimous recommendation of a special committee of disinterested and independent directors, the filing says. The next day, seven holding entities linked to Mark Zuckerberg (CZI Holdings and six Chan Zuckerberg Holdings companies), which together held 56.1 per cent of the voting power, approved it by written consent, according to Meta's preliminary information statement. Record and payment dates for the share dividend will be set later; payment is due no later than the fifth business day after the charter takes effect. Meta expects the Class A price to reflect a two-for-one split once the dividend is paid, and intends to list the Class C shares on Nasdaq.

By our arithmetic, the dividend would create about 2.56bn Class C shares. Meta's quarterly report shows 2,214,472,367 Class A and 341,768,886 Class B shares outstanding on 16 October, which sum to 2,556,241,253, assuming no change before the record date.

The point is control. As of 1 October, Mr Zuckerberg, Meta's founder, chairman and chief executive, beneficially owned 1,220,703 Class A and 341,215,148 Class B shares, about 60.6 per cent of total voting power and about 13.4 per cent of the economic interest, the information statement says. The special committee noted he "is not on the verge of losing voting control". It also recorded that he "would not support a significant equity financing that would have the effect of reducing his voting power", and that its adviser, Moelis, estimated Meta could raise at most about $700bn in equity over several years under the current structure before his voting power fell below 50 per cent.

The more unusual terms sit in a Founder Alignment Agreement signed on 20 October. Twenty per cent of the Class C shares Mr Zuckerberg and his permitted transferees receive will be freely saleable, which the information statement says represents 10 per cent of his overall economic stake. The other 80 per cent are "Stapled Class C Shares": for each one sold, he must within two business days sell a Class A or Class B share, convert a Class B share to Class A, or apply credits from earlier sales. A sale that breaks the rule is treated as an automatic conversion of Class B shares into Class A, which carry one vote rather than ten.

The staple loosens on a schedule. At the end of each calendar quarter in which he stays on the executive team or as executive chair full time, 2.5 per cent of his dividend shares are released; after the 32nd such quarter, the rest go. By our arithmetic, 32 quarters at 2.5 per cent is the full 80 per cent, so eight years of qualifying service unstaples everything. He has also committed, for eight years and absent "material unforeseen circumstances", to live in California or wherever Meta has its headquarters.

The filing is plain about why. If California's Proposition 40, a one-time wealth tax on the 3 November ballot, becomes law, Mr Zuckerberg "could become liable for a significant, multi-billion dollar tax payment", and he told the committee he was evaluating moving his primary residence out of California, the information statement says. The freely saleable shares let him pay "his potential tax liability under California Proposition 40, if enacted into law, without affecting his majority voting control". In a July conversation with the lead independent director, he said that paying the tax by selling Class B shares could cut his voting interest to about 53 per cent.

Further one-for-one Class C dividends follow "Doubling Events", when the 30-day volume-weighted Class C price reaches two, four, eight times and so on the $580.60 Class A price of 10 September, provided the compound annual shareholder return since then is at least 10 per cent. In a merger or tender offer, Mr Zuckerberg and his holder group may not take more, or different, consideration than other holders of the same class. After the dividend, if Meta buys back Class A shares, it must use best efforts to buy back at least as many Class C shares.

The plan came with the third-quarter results. Revenue rose 26 per cent to $64.60bn while costs and expenses rose 68 per cent to $51.61bn, including a $9.89bn charge for an August agreement with 52 attorneys general over teen mental health, according to Meta's results release. Capital expenditure, including finance lease principal, was $34.91bn in the quarter, free cash flow was negative $2.12bn, and Meta guided full-year capital expenditure to $135bn-$145bn.

Susan Li, the chief financial officer, told analysts on Wednesday's call: "We believe this share class will maintain our founder led structure. It will expand our funding tools and give us flexibility to invest behind the unprecedented momentum we are seeing across every part of our business, driven by AI. We do not have any specific financing plans to announce at this time," according to a transcript of the call. Analysts want the shares out quickly. "The sooner the better in our view as it would remove the overhang," Doug Anmuth, an analyst at JPMorgan, said, Yahoo Finance reported; Meta shares fell 2 per cent in premarket trading on Thursday, it said.

There is also a large legal bill. On 25 September a New Mexico jury found Meta liable for more than 43 million violations of the state's Unfair Practices Act in a case brought by the state attorney general, and on Wednesday the court imposed a civil penalty of $25bn with 15 per cent post-judgment interest, the company's 10-Q says. Meta says it has accrued an amount for the matter, intends to appeal and may need to post a bond. Raúl Torrez, New Mexico's attorney general, said: "We may have paid for the construction of schools for the next 100 years," TechCrunch reported, while C.J. Mahoney, Meta's chief legal officer, said the ruling "makes it all but impossible to do business in the state of New Mexico".

Why it matters: Meta has so far paid for its AI build-out from operating cash and debt, and with free cash flow now negative it has created a third route, a tradable equity that costs Mr Zuckerberg no votes. Outside shareholders take all the economic dilution from future issuance, and the stapling terms are what they get in return.
