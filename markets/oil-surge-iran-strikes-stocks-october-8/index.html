Oil at $104, Yields at 5.35%, and Stocks Are Running Out of Excuses

Reports of possible U.S. strikes on Iran sent crude surging and futures sliding Thursday, as a PepsiCo guidance cut and heavy selling in Asia added to the pressure on record-high markets.

Elena Vasquez · Markets Correspondent
October 8, 202621 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Stock futures sank Thursday as a sudden spike in oil prices collided with already-elevated bond yields, giving investors two reasons to sell on the same morning. Futures tracking the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 1 percent, with S&P 500 futures down 0.6 percent and Nasdaq futures off 0.8 percent, after the S&P and Nasdaq pulled back from record closes set on Tuesday.

The trigger was crude. Brent, the global oil benchmark, jumped 4.25 percent to $104.50 a barrel amid reports that the White House is considering new strikes in Iran before the midterm elections, Investopedia reported. Oil futures extended gains as traders priced in the possibility of supply disruption in the world's most geopolitically sensitive energy corridor. Gold futures rose to around $4,136 an ounce, the classic flight bid, while bitcoin slipped to about $82,500, well below the $87,000 highs hit earlier in the week.

The 10-year Treasury yield sat near 5.35 percent, up from 5.29 percent late Wednesday and just below the week's 5.37 percent peak, the highest since 2002. Rising oil prices complicate the picture because they add inflation pressure just as the Federal Reserve is tightening. Minutes from the September meeting showed all 19 officials backed lifting the benchmark rate to 3.75 to 4 percent, the first increase since July 2023.

The earnings backdrop is not helping

PepsiCo topped expectations in its latest quarter but cut its profit forecast, the kind of report that reads as a warning about consumer resilience. Samsung's preliminary third-quarter results left memory investors wanting more, and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index lost 1.2 percent overnight. The one bright spot was Applied Digital, whose shares gained after the data center operator posted a massive jump in revenue, a result some investors read as a bullish signal for the AI trade.

Asia set a grim tone. CNBC TV18 reported that the MSCI Asia Pacific index shed 0.3 percent, with Japan's Nikkei down 1.12 percent, the Topix off 1.42 percent and South Korea's Kospi losing 0.56 percent. Mainland Chinese markets reopened after a holiday to the same wall of worry.

India had one of its worst sessions in weeks. The Sensex plunged 1,045 points, or 1.44 percent, to 71,593, while the Nifty fell 371 points to 22,231, the Hindu BusinessLine reported. The Reserve Bank of India raised its benchmark repo rate by 25 basis points to 5.50 percent on Wednesday, its first hike in nearly four years, signaling that rate cuts are off the table with inflation rising, the rupee weak and crude climbing.

The consumer is the next test

Underneath the macro noise, the earnings season now underway will test whether the consumer can keep carrying the economy. PepsiCo's quarter was the early warning: beating on the top line while cutting the profit outlook is what companies do when volumes are softening and costs are not. With the 30-year mortgage near 7.5 percent and credit card delinquencies ticking up, the spending that powered 2026 has thinner support than the headline numbers suggest.

The Fed's September minutes added an unusual footnote to the week. Alongside the rate decision, they revealed that the Treasury Department, not the Fed, executed the U.S. side of Japan's late-July yen intervention, a reminder that currency markets are back as a policy tool. With the yen near 158 to the dollar and the won's strength already trimming Samsung's translated earnings, the strong dollar is becoming a headwind for the very multinationals the market is counting on.

What to watch

The market's problem is not any single data point. It is the combination: energy shock risk, a Fed that just started hiking again, long-term yields at generational highs, and earnings that are good but not good enough to justify record multiples. The S&P 500's concentration in megacap tech has carried the indexes this far, but Thursday's action showed how quickly the bid can vanish when oil and yields move together.

The White House has not confirmed the Iran strike reporting, and crude could give back its gains as fast as it took them. But the underlying setup, tight energy markets, a hiking Fed and richly valued equities, does not need a geopolitical spark to stay uncomfortable. It just got one anyway.
