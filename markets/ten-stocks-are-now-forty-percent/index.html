Market Structure

Ten stocks are now forty percent

The most widely held index in the world has become a concentrated position, and the mechanism that concentrated it is the buying itself.

Priya Raghavan · Markets Reporter
August 20, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

The index most people mean when they say they own the market has become a concentrated position. The ten largest constituents now account for close to forty percent of its weight, which is roughly double where that figure sat a decade ago.

Analysts tracking the composition describe it plainly as a narrowing of the index rather than a broad advance, with the increase driven almost entirely by the appreciation of a small group of large technology companies.

Concentration in a capitalization-weighted index is not a malfunction. The design is explicit: companies that grow become a larger share of it. Complaining that the winners dominate is complaining about the instrument working.

What is different is the reflexivity introduced by how the index is now owned. Money entering a capitalization-weighted fund is allocated in proportion to existing weights, so a dollar arriving buys the most of whatever is already the largest, regardless of any view about it.

Commentators have described the resulting arrangement as index investing that functions as a bet on ten companies, which overstates the case slightly and identifies the mechanism correctly.

The loop is real but not unbounded. Flows push weights up, higher weights capture more of the next flow, and the process continues until something interrupts it, which historically has been earnings rather than sentiment.

The practical consequence for an ordinary investor is that diversification has quietly changed meaning. A portfolio split across an index fund, a technology sector fund and a growth fund holds substantially the same handful of companies three times, at different fees.

Institutional allocators encounter the same problem in a more explicit form. A mandate to hold the market and a mandate to limit single-name exposure now conflict, and resolving that conflict requires deviating from the benchmark the allocator is measured against.

The concentration also imports a correlation that the index is not supposed to carry. When the largest constituents share a business driver, an index that appears to hold five hundred companies has a meaningful part of its outcome determined by one thesis about one technology.

Equal-weighted and capped alternatives exist and have their own failure mode, which is that they have underperformed for years by systematically owning less of what went up. That is a defensible position and a difficult one to hold through a review meeting.

None of this is a forecast. It is a description of what the instrument currently contains, which has drifted a long way from the thing most holders believe they bought.
