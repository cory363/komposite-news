SpaceX takes its forty billion as a lease

SpaceX has signed a binding commitment for an Apollo-managed entity to buy Nvidia GPU pods and lease them to it, with about $40bn of principal repaid over roughly five years. Three weeks ago the plan was reported as loans and bonds.

Priya Raghavan · House byline
October 26, 20265 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

SpaceX will pay for about $40bn of Nvidia graphics processors through a finance lease from an entity managed by Apollo Global Management, not the bank loans and bonds first reported, according to a filing the company made on Monday. The lessor will buy the chips and rent them to SpaceX for about five years.

The filing sets out more than the headline figure. SpaceX says that on Saturday it signed a binding commitment letter to enter a master finance lease under which the lessor "will acquire and lease to the Company certain graphics processing unit pods and related equipment" for data centres SpaceX chooses. The equipment arrives in tranches, each on its own schedule lasting "approximately five to five and one-quarter years". Rent is paid quarterly in advance and is made up of one-twentieth of the tranche's financed cost, interest at a fixed annual rate on the outstanding balance, and certain costs. SpaceX expects principal payments across all the schedules to total about $40bn.

Two things are not yet settled. The fixed rate "will be determined at pricing", which happens before SpaceX signs the lease itself, so the interest cost is not known. And the lease is subject to "certain specified closing conditions"; SpaceX expects it to close before the year-end, with the schedules starting in the first half of 2027 as the chips are delivered. The 8-K is signed by Bret Johnsen, the chief financial officer.

A SpaceX release, published on Apollo's website, says the financing was committed by a group led by Apollo and Goldman Sachs, which was sole structuring agent, with anchor commitments from funds and accounts managed by BlackRock and Goldman Sachs Asset Management and capital accounts advised by KKR. It says the lease will deliver Nvidia's Vera Rubin chips for SpaceX's "terrestrial compute buildout" and calls it "the largest committed GPU financing". In commentary attributed to the chief financial officer, the company said: "As demand for compute continues to accelerate, we're seeing less than one year payback periods on our SI infrastructure investments. This lease diversifies our funding sources and addresses a notable portion of the funding required to deliver on our growth plans." SI is SpaceX's shorthand for its Super Intelligence segment, which includes Grok. The payback claim is the company's own and was not independently verified.

The deal that was signed is not the deal that was reported. On 6 October Reuters, citing the Financial Times, said SpaceX planned about $10bn of bank loans and $30bn of investment-grade debt, with Apollo expected to lead and a close expected in 2027. As Komposite reported at the time, the investment-grade label was the point: it would open the deal to insurers and pension funds. Eighteen days later, by our count, the money has arrived as a lease, with a wider lead group and an earlier close.

The lease terms allow some arithmetic of our own. Repaying one-twentieth of the financed cost each quarter clears it in 20 quarters, or five years, which matches the stated term. On the full $40bn that is $2bn of principal a quarter, or $8bn a year, before any interest, once every tranche is running; because the tranches start as chips are delivered, the payments will build up rather than begin at once. For scale, SpaceX's Super Intelligence segment reported revenue of $7.56bn in the three months to 30 September, according to its third-quarter earnings release.

The same release shows debt and finance leases of $38.8bn on 30 September, by our addition of the current and non-current lines. Another $40bn of lease principal would roughly double that, to about $78.8bn. Capital expenditure was $19.6bn in the third quarter and $48.1bn in the first nine months, against $13.1bn of operating cash flow over the same nine months, a gap of about $35bn on our subtraction. Mr Johnsen told analysts on 20 October that the company would spend $35bn to $40bn on capital investment in the fourth quarter alone, TechCrunch reported; on our addition that puts the year at $83bn to $88bn. SpaceX held $51.3bn of cash at the end of September.

The commitment comes as lenders grow more selective about the sector. TechCrunch, reporting the deal, described a turbulent stretch for AI infrastructure debt, and said Elon Musk had argued on X that debt markets were closing to companies without large revenue beyond AI data centres, predicting "guppy-level death and consolidation" among the more than 200 so-called neoclouds that rent out GPUs. On Saturday Apollo's own chief economist, Torsten Slok, wrote that AI had delivered no financial payoff to corporate America outside the technology sector, as Komposite reported on Sunday.

Why it matters: a finance lease lets SpaceX spread a chip order of this size over the period it expects to use the chips, and it draws on private-credit and asset-management money rather than a public bond sale. The cost is a fixed charge that runs whether or not the compute is sold. The lease will sit beside the company's existing debt, and the rate, still to be set, will show what lenders charge to fund chips at this scale.

SpaceX is meanwhile weighing a far larger transaction. Special committees of independent directors at SpaceX and Tesla are considering a combination of the two companies, and Mr Musk, chief executive of both, has said that as a conflicted party he cannot take part in their discussions, as Komposite reported last week.
