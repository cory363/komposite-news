The AI payoff stops at seven companies

Torsten Slok, Apollo's chief economist, says AI has delivered no financial payoff to corporate America outside tech. In a note dated Saturday, he finds margins and revenue per worker flat for the S&P 500 minus its seven biggest technology names.

Priya Raghavan · Markets Reporter
October 25, 20263 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Artificial intelligence has "delivered zero financial payoff to corporate America outside of tech," Torsten Slok, chief economist at Apollo Global Management, the alternative asset manager, wrote in a note dated Saturday. His evidence is a split inside the S&P 500: profit margins and revenue per worker are rising at the seven largest technology companies and flat at the rest.

The note, published in Apollo's Daily Spark series under the title "No Signs of AI Payoff Outside the Tech Sector", compares the so-called Magnificent Seven with what Mr Slok calls the S&P 493. TechCrunch, which reported the note on Sunday and was once owned by Apollo, listed the excluded seven as Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia and Tesla. Mr Slok's first chart shows revenue per worker, adjusted for inflation to the fourth quarter of 2019, rising for the seven and flat for the 493. A second shows the same pattern for profit margins. Both draw on Bloomberg and Macrobond data, according to the chart notes.

A third chart looks for AI in the economy-wide numbers. It uses the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco's utilisation-adjusted total factor productivity series, a measure of output growth not explained by more labour or capital, adjusted for how intensively firms use their existing capacity, with Bureau of Labor Statistics data. Mr Slok wrote that there are "still no signs of AI boosting productivity growth". The note gives no figures in the text for any of the series; it presents them as charts.

The last two charts concern spending on tokens, the units in which AI model providers bill for usage, and draw on Ramp, the corporate card and spend management company, its chief economist Ara Kharazian and the Ramp AI Index. Mr Slok writes that venture-backed companies are spending more and more on tokens, but that "mainstream corporate America does not need the latest model and is unwilling to pay for it". With no impact on the bottom line, he says, those companies are pulling back and "starting to view token spending not as a source of growth but as a cost center that weighs on margins". As long as agentic AI and token spending are confined to a small part of the economy, he concludes, the macroeconomic effects of AI will be "very limited".

He is also sceptical that cheaper intelligence will change that soon. The question, he wrote, is whether mainstream companies "can turn increasingly cheap intelligence into higher revenue growth and higher profit margins", adding: "AI may be a great calculator, but in business, intelligence is mostly judgment." The note argues that AI "does not understand accountability, trust, culture, unwritten rules, ethical trade-offs or fairness", factors he says shape reputational risk, revenue growth and margins. It ends with the line that "the AI story will be decided in the S&P 493, not the Mag 7".

The argument has limits. It shows that gains have not appeared in aggregate margins and revenue per worker, which is not the same as showing that no company outside tech has benefited. It is one economist's reading of public and third-party data at an investment firm, not a peer-reviewed study, and Apollo's disclaimer says the views do not necessarily reflect the firm's. TechCrunch noted that recent studies have also found AI has yet to deliver significant productivity gains, and the cost side is visible elsewhere: McKinsey's State of AI survey found one in five organisations saying AI operating costs constrain their use of it.

Why it matters: the AI build-out is being financed on the expectation that customers outside the technology sector will eventually pay for it in volume. If the companies buying AI services cannot show a return, the revenue assumptions behind data-centre spending carry more risk, a question that has run through this quarter's earnings coverage. Komposite has tracked that question from the supplier side, including an internal OpenAI projection of $278 billion of negative free cash flow through 2030, and from the buyer side, in finance chiefs demanding measurable returns on AI projects.
