Forty-one billion raised, nine billion short

Alphabet spent $51.4bn on data centres in the third quarter, more than its operating cash flow, and covered the gap with $28.7bn of bonds and $12.4bn of new shares. Microsoft, reporting the same evening, paid for a doubled build-out from its own cash.

Priya Raghavan · House byline
October 29, 20264 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Alphabet's free cash flow was minus $9.1bn in the third quarter after it spent $51.4bn on property and equipment, a gap it covered with $28.7bn of bonds and $12.4bn of new shares, its results release on Wednesday shows. Microsoft, reporting the same evening, doubled its own spending and still generated $18.6bn of free cash.

Alphabet's revenue rose 25 per cent to $127.6bn in the three months to 30 September. Operating cash flow fell to $42.3bn from $48.4bn a year earlier, while purchases of property and equipment rose to $51.4bn from $24.0bn. The company made no share buybacks in the quarter, against $11.5bn a year earlier, and paid $2.9bn in dividends. It issued $28.7bn of senior unsecured notes and sold 12m Class A and 24m Class C shares through an at-the-market programme for $12.4bn, with $27.6bn of that programme still available at the end of September. Long-term debt stood at $125.1bn, up from $46.5bn at the end of 2025.

It is the second quarter running in which Alphabet has spent more than it generated. Free cash flow was minus $5.9bn in the second quarter, the first negative quarter since the company listed in 2004, InvestingCube reported at the time. On the release's own measure, trailing twelve-month free cash flow has fallen to $19.7bn. The company kept its 2026 capital spending forecast at $195bn to $205bn and said spending in 2027 will "increase significantly", The Motley Fool reported.

Microsoft's numbers show the other way to pay for the same thing. Its fiscal first-quarter release shows revenue up 19 per cent to $92.3bn, Azure cloud revenue up 48 per cent and additions to property and equipment of $39.4bn, up from $19.4bn. Operating cash flow rose to $58.0bn, so free cash flow, by our arithmetic, was $18.6bn, down 27 per cent from $25.7bn a year earlier but positive. Microsoft issued no debt, repaid $4.9bn and bought back $4.4bn of its shares. Including finance leases, its capital spending was $45.2bn, The Motley Fool reported.

"We had a strong quarter and are now on a half-trillion dollar revenue run rate," Sundar Pichai, Alphabet's chief executive, said in the release. Investors read it differently. Alphabet's shares were down 10.3 per cent at $291.50 before the open on Thursday, Investing.com reported, and were down about 13 per cent later in the session, putting the stock on course for its worst day since its 2004 listing and nearly $500bn of market value at stake, Yahoo Finance reported. Microsoft "held up better", Yahoo said, and the Nasdaq Composite fell more than 1 per cent.

The earnings line was weaker too. Diluted earnings per share fell to $2.12 from $2.87, against an analyst consensus of roughly $3.02 cited by Investing.com, partly because of an $8.7bn loss on equity securities, which Investing.com attributed mainly to Alphabet's stake in SpaceX. Google Cloud was the exception: revenue rose 103 per cent to $30.7bn and operating income to $11.1bn. Brent Thill, an analyst at Jefferies, said cloud was "the only business that beat" and "everything else missed", according to Investing.com.

The share sales came at higher prices than Thursday's. By our arithmetic, Alphabet's at-the-market sales raised an average of roughly $358 for each Class A share and $338 for each Class C share, net of costs, against $291.50 before Thursday's open. A year earlier, Alphabet was spending $11.5bn a quarter buying its shares back; in the third quarter it was a net seller. Capital spending equal to 121 per cent of operating cash flow, against 68 per cent at Microsoft, is the gap the market priced on Thursday.

Why it matters: the largest AI builders are now splitting into those that can fund data centres from operations and those that need the capital markets. Meta priced $37.5bn of new equity on Monday after its own free cash flow turned negative, Komposite reported, and Apollo's chief economist has argued that the financial payoff from AI stops at seven companies. Alphabet's cloud business, which is now renting its chips through Oracle, is the part of the company growing fast enough to justify the spending; the question its shareholders asked on Thursday is how long the rest has to pay for it.
