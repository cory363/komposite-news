The commission goes to court

One app store settled and cut its rate. The other is defending its remaining fee at the Supreme Court, which will decide what a platform may charge for a link.

Jonathan Bright · Policy Editor
September 6, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

The long argument about app store commissions has split into two different endings. One platform settled and lowered its rate. The other is defending what remains of its fee at the Supreme Court.

The settlement came first and moved further than most observers expected. Reporting on the agreement describes a reduction from the long-standing thirty percent standard alongside a route for developers to use their own billing.

The litigation took the opposite path. The Supreme Court agreed to review a set of decisions finding that the platform had defied an earlier injunction about developer fees, after a contempt finding in the lower courts.

The question in that case is narrower than the coverage implies and more consequential than it sounds. It is not whether developers may tell users about cheaper prices elsewhere; a court settled that. It is whether the platform may charge a commission when they do.

That distinction is the whole economics. A right to link out that carries a fee close to the in-app rate changes nothing, because the developer saves nothing by exercising it. The fee is what determines whether the remedy exists in practice.

Developers have been living in the gap while it is resolved. External payment links have been permitted at no commission during parts of the dispute, which produced a brief and unusual window in which the alternative was genuinely cheaper.

The result is a compliance environment that varies by platform and by region, with European rules operating on their own schedule, so a single app now faces different distribution and payment rules depending on where it is installed.

For smaller developers the practical calculation has not changed as much as the headlines suggest. Running an independent payment path means handling tax, refunds, fraud and support, and the platform fee buys all of that.

Which is the argument the platforms have always made, and it is not a bad one. The dispute is whether the price is set by a market or by an owner who also controls the only shelf.

A decision either way will not end the matter. If the fee survives, the pressure moves to legislatures; if it does not, the pressure moves to whatever the platforms charge for the things a commission used to cover.
