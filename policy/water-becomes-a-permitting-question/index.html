Water becomes a permitting question

Legislators have moved from asking data centres about electricity to asking them about water, and the disclosure requests are arriving first.

Jonathan Bright · Policy Editor
September 6, 20262 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

The political argument about data centres has been about electricity for two years. It is turning toward water, which is a harder conversation because water is local, visible and already allocated.

Legislative attention is arriving as disclosure rather than prohibition. Counsel summarising a state committee hearing describe interest in monthly water-use reporting, source disclosure and drought contingency planning, alongside discussion of restricting evaporative cooling.

That sequence is familiar and deliberate. A reporting requirement is easier to pass than a limit, it produces the data a limit would need, and it shifts the argument from estimates to filings.

The estimates are what created the pressure. Large facilities can consume volumes comparable to a small town, and in places where agriculture and municipal supply already compete, an additional industrial draw is a visible allocation decision.

Evaporative cooling is the specific practice under scrutiny because it is the one that consumes rather than circulates. Water used to reject heat by evaporation does not return to the system, which is what distinguishes it from cooling that merely borrows.

Alternatives exist and the trade is well understood inside the industry. Closed-loop and air-cooled designs use dramatically less water and more electricity, which moves the burden onto a grid that is also constrained.

That trade is the reason the policy question is genuinely hard rather than merely contested. A jurisdiction can protect its water and increase its power problem, and the two constraints have different constituencies and different regulators.

Siting decisions have already begun reflecting it. Operators are weighing water availability alongside interconnection timelines, and the places with abundant power and scarce water are no longer automatically attractive.

The disclosure regimes will also produce a comparison nobody has had before. Once monthly figures exist per facility, an operator using far more than a comparable neighbour has a question to answer that no press release will settle.

Which is the point of starting with reporting. It converts a dispute about plausible numbers into a dispute about published ones, and that is a different negotiation.
