The stablecoin rule that will not say what a reward is

The OCC on Wednesday finalised its GENIUS Act rule for stablecoin issuers, effective 18 January 2027. It dropped a proposed presumption against reward arrangements and declined to define interest or yield, leaving each programme to its facts.

Jonathan Bright · House byline
October 21, 20263 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC), the federal regulator of national banks, on Wednesday finalised its rule implementing the GENIUS Act for payment stablecoin issuers, dropping a proposed presumption against reward arrangements. The rule and the Act take effect on 18 January 2027, the agency said in its announcement.

The rule covers permitted payment stablecoin issuers and foreign issuers under the OCC's jurisdiction, and custody by the banks it supervises. Bulletin 2026-50 says most of it sits in a new Part 15 of Title 12 of the Code of Federal Regulations, covering activities, reserve assets, redemption, risk management, audits, custody, applications, revocation and a capital and operational backstop. Anti-money-laundering, Bank Secrecy Act and sanctions rules are left to a separate rulemaking with the Treasury.

"Today's final rule advances President Trump and Secretary Bessent's vision for American leadership in digital assets," said Jonathan Gould, the Comptroller. "We are providing a clear, practical framework for payment stablecoin innovation, with strong standards for reserve backing, redemption, and risk management." Scott Bessent, the Treasury Secretary, compared the Act to the National Currency Act of 1863 in the same release.

The contested point is yield. The Act bars issuers from paying holders "any form of interest or yield" solely in connection with holding, using or retaining a stablecoin. The OCC's proposal, published on 2 March, would have presumed that certain arrangements with affiliates and related third parties broke that ban. The final rule says the OCC "has determined to eliminate the rebuttable presumption", and that it "will apply principles of statutory interpretation" to the terms "in light of the facts and circumstances before it", rather than define interest or yield.

The word "solely" carries much of the weight. The rule says it "is best read under its plain terms to exclude payments made in connection with reasons other than holding, using, or retaining a payment stablecoin", so "payments made for other purposes are not barred". Transaction-based payments that are not interest or yield, or not made solely for holding or use, "would not be prohibited", it says, while the OCC will look through payments made "only nominally for another purpose" to evade the ban.

It gives no safe harbours. The OCC "declines to presume that rewards programs that predated the GENIUS Act comply", and will judge them the same as later ones. It declined a safe harbour for white-label partners, whose payments "may be more likely to be made on behalf of" an issuer. Payments for bona fide services "generally would not be prohibited". On pending legislation, it says: "Unless and until such legislation passes, the OCC must implement the GENIUS Act's yield prohibition, as enacted."

The banks' argument is recorded but not adopted. Commenters favouring a strict ban cited reports that deposits "could be reduced by trillions of dollars", the rule notes, particularly at community banks. American Banker read the final text as leaving room for yield manoeuvres, and CoinDesk as a fresh start for weighing rewards programmes against the ban.

The OCC is one of several agencies writing rules under the Act. Treasury proposed its rules on 18 August and the Federal Reserve on 24 September; CoinDesk reported that the OCC's is the first final rule from a federal banking regulator. The OCC said it will announce separately when it begins accepting applications and waiver requests.

Why it matters: by declining to define a reward in advance, the OCC has left exchanges, wallet providers and issuers to design programmes at their own risk, with supervision and enforcement setting the boundary case by case. Komposite compared the agencies' proposals in "The charter picks the arithmetic", which noted that none was final at the time.
