$30.6m in reserve against $1.8m

The AI industry's two super PAC networks had spent $34.5m and $25.2m on independent expenditures by their latest federal filings. The network funded by investors including Andreessen Horowitz's founders held $30.6m in cash; the Anthropic-backed one held $1.8m.

Jonathan Bright · House byline
November 3, 20264 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

The super PAC network funded by AI investors including Andreessen Horowitz's founders and OpenAI's president held $30.6m in cash in mid-October, while the Anthropic-backed network that opposes it held $1.8m at the end of September, by Komposite's arithmetic on Federal Election Commission filings. Voters cast ballots in the US midterm elections on Tuesday. Between them, the two networks had reported $59.8m of independent expenditures, the advertising and other spending a super PAC makes for or against candidates without coordinating with them.

The larger network is built around Leading the Future, a super PAC registered in Nevada. Its pre-general report, which runs to 14 October, shows $76.0m of receipts, almost all of it $75.1m in contributions, and no independent expenditures of its own. Instead it passed $47.0m to two affiliated super PACs, Think Big and American Mission, which received $24.5m and $22.5m from other committees and spent $18.5m and $16.1m on independent expenditures. By our arithmetic, 61.9 per cent of what Leading the Future took in has gone to the two, and the three committees between them held $30.6m on 14 October, of which $23.8m sat with Leading the Future itself.

The two networks' figures run to different dates, 14 October and 30 September, so the comparison is not like for like. Independent expenditures reported after those dates, including in 24-hour reports, are not included.

The rival network is smaller in cash and less visible in its funding. Anthropic said in February that it was giving $20m to Public First Action, which it described as "a new bipartisan 501(c)(4)" that would "support public education about AI, promote safeguards, and ensure America leads the AI race". A 501(c)(4) is a nonprofit that may engage in some political activity and does not have to disclose its donors. Its affiliated super PACs do file with the FEC: Public First, which took in $4.9m in 2026 and passed $4.34m to other committees; Jobs and Democracy PAC, which reported $17.9m of independent expenditures through September; and Defending Our Values PAC, with $7.4m. Defending Our Values would run ads supporting Senator Pete Ricketts of Nebraska, who is seeking re-election, Bloomberg reported in February.

Who funds Leading the Future is a matter of record. Greg Brockman, OpenAI's president, and his wife each gave $12.5m in 2025, as did Marc Andreessen and Ben Horowitz, the co-founders of the venture firm Andreessen Horowitz, The Washington Sun reported from the committee's filings; the investor Joe Lonsdale, the investor Ron Conway and the AI search company Perplexity were also donors. The network said in January that it had raised $125m in the second half of 2025, while its super PAC's filing showed $50.3m. Its contributions have reached $75.1m since, so by our subtraction it has reported $24.8m of new contributions in 2026. Leading the Future also gave $5m to a nonprofit arm, Build American AI, which does not have to disclose its donors, the paper reported.

The Leading the Future group's spending is also the subject of a complaint. The Campaign Legal Center, a campaign-finance watchdog, told the FEC in May that Think Big and American Mission paid virtually all of their funds to newly formed companies, and that "neither LLC appears to be a bona fide commercial vendor operating at arm's length", according to its complaint. Komposite found no public FEC ruling on the complaint.

The figures carry two caveats. First, the dates differ: the Leading the Future committees' pre-general reports run to 14 October, while the Public First committees' latest reports on the FEC's site run to 30 September. Second, super PACs report independent expenditures of $1,000 or more within 24 hours in the last 20 days before an election, so both networks have spent money since that is not in these totals. The cash figures are the best public measure of what each side had left for the final weeks, not of what it held on Tuesday.

What the networks want is on record too. Leading the Future's stated goal is a "national regulatory framework for AI", which The Washington Sun described as a way to block state enforcement. Anthropic listed opposing the pre-emption of state laws without stronger federal safeguards among the priorities of the group it funds, and said it would work with "Republicans, Democrats, and Independents". Public First Action had not disclosed what it raised beyond Anthropic's $20m, Bloomberg reported in February. The question of who writes AI rules has run through Komposite's coverage this autumn, from eight state attorneys general suing OpenAI to the AI companies testifying before New York's city council.

Why it matters: both networks were set up to fight over how AI is regulated, and the disclosed money behind each comes largely from a handful of AI companies, investors and founders. The outcomes of the races they spent on are not yet known. When they are, the filings will show how much each side spent per race, and what it kept back.
