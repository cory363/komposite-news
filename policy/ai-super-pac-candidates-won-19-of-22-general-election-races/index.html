Nineteen of twenty-two, mostly in safe seats

Candidates backed by the Leading the Future super PACs won 19 of the 22 general-election races the network spent on, by our match of FEC filings to AP results. Fifteen won by more than ten points. Most of both AI networks' money went on primaries.

Jonathan Bright · House byline
November 5, 20267 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Candidates backed by the AI industry's largest super PAC network won 19 of the 22 general-election races it spent on, by Komposite's match of Federal Election Commission filings to Associated Press results. One lost and two were uncalled on Thursday morning. Fifteen of the 19 winners won by more than ten percentage points.

The network is built around Leading the Future, a super PAC funded by investors including the founders of Andreessen Horowitz and OpenAI's president, Greg Brockman, which passes its money to two affiliates, Think Big and American Mission. Its rival, the Public First network, whose nonprofit arm received $20m from Anthropic, spends through Jobs and Democracy PAC and Defending Our Values PAC. Both networks were set up to fight over how AI is regulated, as Komposite reported on Election Day. That story ended on a promise: once results were in, the filings would show what each side had spent per race. This is that count.

How did we count it?

We used the FEC's own data and the AP's results, and the inputs are public. On Thursday at 09:16 Eastern time we downloaded the FEC's bulk file of 24- and 48-hour independent-expenditure reports for the 2026 cycle. An independent expenditure is spending, usually on advertising, mail or texts, that calls for a candidate's election or defeat without being coordinated with any campaign. We kept the 447 unique transactions reported by the four committees that do the networks' spending, replaced amended entries with the latest version, and added them up by candidate, by whether the money supported or opposed that candidate, and by the election each filer said it was for. We then matched each general-election candidate to the AP's calls and vote shares as published in NPR's Senate and House results data, which we checked at 09:19 Eastern time.

How much of the money went to the general election?

Less than a seventh of it. The four committees had reported $59.3m of independent expenditures when we checked, and $7.9m of that, 13.3 per cent, was marked for the general election. The rest went on primaries, runoffs and special elections. The timing shows the same pattern: the Leading the Future network's general-election items are dated from 28 May to 16 October, and the Public First network's from 20 to 23 October, so neither had reported any general-election spending dated in the final ten days before the vote. The Leading the Future network put 19.5 per cent of its money into the general; the Public First network put in 4.6 per cent. The single most expensive contest for both was a primary. In New York's 12th district, Jobs and Democracy PAC spent $13.2m supporting Alex Bores, a state assemblyman, and Think Big spent $8.2m opposing him. That one race took 35.9 per cent of everything the two networks reported. Bores finished second to Micah Lasher on 23 June, with 35.0 per cent of the vote to Lasher's 39.0 per cent on unofficial counts, PoliticsNY reported.

How did the Leading the Future candidates do?

Nineteen won, one lost and two were still being counted. The network supported 22 general-election candidates and opposed none: American Mission backed 17 Republicans with $5.46m and Think Big backed five Democrats with $1.34m. By our arithmetic, $4.56m, or 67.1 per cent of its general-election money, went to candidates who won, and $1.60m, 23.6 per cent, to the one who lost: Senator Darline Graham of South Carolina, the network's largest single general-election investment, who lost to Annie Andrews by 51.1 per cent to 46.7 per cent. In Mississippi, where the network spent $494,528 for Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith, the AP had not called the race; Scott Colom led her by 1,138 votes. California's 23rd district, where it spent $140,448 for Representative Jay Obernolte, was also uncalled, with Obernolte ahead. The three candidates who were not incumbents, Letlow, Andy Barr in Kentucky and Kurt Alme in Montana, all won Senate seats that their party already held.

Were these competitive races?

Mostly not. Nineteen of the 22 candidates were incumbents, according to the AP's data, and 15 of the 19 winners won by more than ten points. Only seven of the 22 races were decided by fewer than ten points or remained uncalled: Louisiana's Senate race, which Julia Letlow won by 1.8 points; three House seats in Indiana and Florida that the backed Republicans held by between 5.1 and 8.6 points; and South Carolina, Mississippi and California's 23rd. Those seven took $3.34m, or 49.1 per cent of the network's general-election money, and so far show four wins, one loss and two uncalled. The other half went to the 15 candidates who won by more than ten points, in amounts of $199,000 to $351,000 each, plus $5,000 for one. A record built mostly on safe incumbents says little about whether the spending changed any result, and we do not claim that it did.

What did the Public First network do?

It spent in three general-election races, all involving Republicans, for $1.11m in total. Defending Our Values PAC supported John Cowan in Georgia's 11th district, who won, and Alex Mealer in Texas's 9th, who lost to Leticia Gutierrez. Jobs and Democracy PAC spent $599,445 opposing Representative Gabe Evans in Colorado's 8th district, who lost to Manny Rutinel. The outcome matched the network's position in two of the three races.

What does the count leave out?

Three things. First, the source is the 24- and 48-hour reports, which cover independent expenditures of $10,000 or more at any time and of $1,000 or more in the last 20 days before an election. Smaller earlier items appear only in periodic reports, which is why our total for the Public First network, $24.4m, is below the $25.2m its committees had reported through September. Second, no report from these committees filed after 28 October had appeared in the FEC's data when we checked, so any later spending is missing. Third, the results are unofficial counts, and the AP had not called every race. We did not assess each candidate's position on AI, and the networks' choice of candidates is their own.

What the networks say they want is on record. Leading the Future's spokesperson, Jesse Hunt, said in January that the group planned to "back leaders who will set aside politics to establish a clear, consistent national framework", The Washington Sun reported. Brad Carson, Public First's founder and a former Democratic congressman, told the paper: "We have $50 million and 85% of the public sentiment." We found no statement from either network on the general-election results by Thursday morning.

Why it matters: the two networks were the AI industry's most direct attempt to shape who writes its rules, and their own filings show where the effort went. Most of it was spent before the autumn, in primaries such as New York's 12th, rather than on the races decided on Tuesday. In the general election, the larger network mostly backed incumbents who were already favoured. How the new Congress handles AI, including whether federal rules should override state laws such as the 31 state election deepfake laws on the books this week, is the test both networks were funded for.
