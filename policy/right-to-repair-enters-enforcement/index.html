Right to Repair

Right to repair enters enforcement

The statutes passed. The fight moved to parts pairing, diagnostic software, and what a manufacturer may require before a repair will function.

Jonathan Bright · Policy Editor
September 1, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Right to repair spent a decade as a question of whether legislatures would act. That question closed. What replaced it is narrower, more technical, and considerably harder for manufacturers to answer.

The contested ground is parts pairing: the practice of using software to check whether a replacement component was supplied or authorized by the manufacturer, and degrading or disabling the device when it was not. A part can be genuine, correctly fitted and functional, and still be refused by the machine it was installed in.

Legislative drafting has followed the practice. Advocates tracking the bills describe model language that now addresses software-based restrictions directly, rather than only requiring that parts and manuals be sold, on the reasoning that availability means nothing if the device declines to accept what was bought.

The volume is the part manufacturers underestimated. More than thirty bills were introduced across a dozen states in the opening weeks of this year alone, which means compliance planning can no longer wait for a particular statute to pass in a particular place.

Effective dates and enforcement dates have also come apart, which creates a window that is easy to misread. Colorado's restrictions on parts pairing took effect at the start of this year while enforcement does not begin until next, and Texas became the eighth state with a consumer electronics repair law when its statute took effect on September 1.

Counsel advising manufacturers describe the resulting obligation as a patchwork with common structural features: parts, tools, diagnostic software and documentation supplied to owners and independent shops on fair and reasonable terms, with the definitions doing most of the work.

Diagnostic software is where the arguments concentrate, because it is the one deliverable that is genuinely hard to hand over. Parts can be inventoried and manuals can be published. A diagnostic tool is a live piece of software that talks to a device, receives updates, and often carries the same authentication that gates repair in the first place.

Manufacturers argue that opening those tools creates a safety and security surface, and in some product categories that argument is substantive rather than tactical. Medical devices, vehicles at speed, and anything carrying a large battery are not cases where an unauthenticated write path is a small matter.

Repair advocates answer that the same argument was made about manuals, then about parts, and that the security case tends to expand to cover whatever was most recently required. Where the exceptions land will determine whether the statutes change practice or generate paperwork.

Independent shops are already reporting the second-order effect, which is that compliance is arriving unevenly by product line rather than by company. A manufacturer subject to an automotive statute in one state and an electronics statute in another tends to comply narrowly, product by product, rather than adopting one policy.

That is the shape of the next several years. The principle is settled and the schedules are published. What remains is a long argument about firmware, conducted one product category at a time, in states that will not finish at the same time or agree on the terms.
