Megaspeed's paper trail, from Commerce to the prosecutors

The Commerce Department's chip-smuggling inquiry into Nvidia customer Megaspeed was paused at the Justice Department's request after an Nvidia representative contacted investigators, NBC News reported. Our timeline puts that beside a year of letters, indictments and filings.

Jonathan Bright · House byline
November 10, 20265 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

The Justice Department asked the Commerce Department to pause its part of an investigation into whether a Singapore cloud company smuggled Nvidia's AI chips into China, and the request came after an Nvidia representative reached out to Commerce investigators, NBC News reported on Monday evening, citing a person familiar with the matter. The company was Megaspeed, the Nvidia customer whose executive Alice Huang is at the centre of the inquiry Nvidia's board opened on Sunday.

Bloomberg News reported the same day that Nvidia's general counsel, Tim Teter, had contacted Jeffrey Kessler, the Commerce under secretary who oversees the Bureau of Industry and Security, in the late summer of 2025 about the Megaspeed probe, and that the Justice Department asked the bureau to halt its work late that year. We could not read Bloomberg's report in the original; those points are taken from two separate published summaries of it that agree on them. According to those accounts, Nvidia described its contacts with officials as routine, and the bureau said it handles law-enforcement matters in line with its rules. No charges against Megaspeed have been made public.

Megaspeed denies wrongdoing. Its website cites inspections by the Bureau of Industry and Security, Malaysia's trade ministry and Nvidia that found "no evidence of any violations concerning the illicit transfer of chips or any illegal activities", CNBC reported on Monday. Nvidia's position for a year has been that the diversion allegations were investigated and disproved. In October 2025 it described Megaspeed as "running a small commercial cloud, like many other companies throughout the world, as allowed by U.S. export control rules", CNBC reported at the time.

Put in order, the public record shows how long the question has been open, and how many other people asked it. Komposite assembled the documents below from Congress, the Justice Department and Nvidia's own filings. The first official confirmation of an inquiry, from Singapore's police, came 396 days ago. Three days later Senator Tom Cotton, a Republican from Arkansas, wrote to the commerce secretary, Howard Lutnick, asking him to investigate. "Megaspeed recently received billions of dollars of U.S. chips, potentially violating U.S. export laws," his letter said. It set no deadline.

The three Justice Department cases in the table were brought in the months around that pause. Each named the Bureau of Industry and Security among its investigating agencies. A Florida indictment of November 2025 alleged that 400 Nvidia A100 chips were sent to China by way of Malaysia and Thailand. A Texas case announced in December 2025 alleged exports or attempted exports of at least $160m of H100 and H200 chips. In March this year, prosecutors in Manhattan charged three men, including a senior executive of a US server maker, with diverting at least about $510m of servers loaded with restricted Nvidia products to China. One of them, a contractor, has since pleaded guilty, as Komposite reported. None of the three cases names Megaspeed, and defendants who have not pleaded are presumed innocent.

Those cases were the basis for a letter of 1 June from Senator Elizabeth Warren, the senior Democrat on the Banking Committee. It was addressed to two people: Teter, the same general counsel, and Brooke Seawell, who chairs Nvidia's audit committee. It asked four questions, among them whether the audit committee had evaluated Nvidia's export-control compliance after the server indictment, and what evidence supported executives' public statements that there was "no evidence" of diversion. The letter said the cases "raise urgent questions about whether NVIDIA's Board of Directors is exercising meaningful oversight" and asked for answers by 18 June, which by our count was 145 days ago.

Nvidia's own disclosures say little either way. The legal-proceedings note in its most recent quarterly report, filed on 26 August, describes only shareholder suits over sales to cryptocurrency miners in 2017 and 2018, and says no liabilities are accrued for them. It names no export-control inquiry. Its accounting note refers in general terms to "regulatory and government inquiries and investigations". As of Tuesday morning, Nvidia's EDGAR filings showed no Form 8-K about the board's inquiry or about the Commerce reports, by our check; the latest filing was a director's Form 4 on 6 November.

Why it matters: the reports turn a question about one customer into a question about how Nvidia deals with the agency that licenses its exports. Contact between companies and their regulators is normal, and nothing published so far shows that Nvidia's outreach changed the investigation's course. But the timing now sits on the record beside a year of warnings from both parties in Congress, and Nvidia's board said on Sunday that its independent directors would examine "all available information", as Komposite reported. The export-control system has been moving from writing rules to enforcing them, which makes the handling of the largest enforcement question in the industry harder to set aside.

The calendar is short. The three House chairmen who asked Jensen Huang to brief their committees wanted a reply by Wednesday and proposed 18 November for the briefing. Nvidia reports third-quarter results the day before, on 17 November. Both are the first places Nvidia can be asked directly about what its representatives told Commerce, and when.
