Twenty-five thousand posts, and no number for AI

The Dutch government plans to cut about 25,000 full-time posts from its central civil service by 2035 to save €3.5bn a year. The letter announcing it warns against overestimating what AI will do, and puts no figure on it until 2027.

Jonathan Bright · House byline
October 29, 20264 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

The Dutch government plans to cut about 25,000 full-time posts from its central civil service by 2035, but the letter announcing it on Monday puts no figure on how many jobs artificial intelligence will replace. State Secretary Eric van der Burg sent it to parliament. The cut, about one post in six, is meant to save €900m in 2027, rising to €3.5bn a year from 2035, the public broadcaster NOS reported.

The letter, a progress report on the cabinet's "Slagvaardige Overheid" (effective government) programme sent to the lower house on 26 October, counts 160,016 full-time equivalents in central government and calls the 25,000 figure a rough estimate, according to AI Insider, a Dutch trade site that published extracts. Police and Defence are exempt, as are the prison service, the Public Prosecution Service and the security services. The benefits agency UWV, the immigration service IND and the tax administration were not exempted in advance, NOS said. Many organisations face cuts of about 20 per cent, some about 45 per cent and one about 60 per cent.

The international framing was simpler. Bloomberg described an "AI-led efficiency drive" and quoted Mr van der Burg: "We cannot keep doing what we have been doing," and the goal is a central government that is "simpler, smarter, and smaller". Bloomberg also reported that parliament had passed non-binding motions calling for a 20 per cent productivity increase through AI, and that the cabinet would present plans for deploying the technology early next year.

The letter is more cautious than the headlines. "Overschat de formatieve effecten van digitalisering en AI niet," it says, as quoted by AI Insider: do not overestimate the staffing effects of digitalisation and AI. It sets no AI target until the first quarter of 2027, when the house has asked for one, and says security, data quality and sovereignty must be in order, infrastructure adapted and work processes fundamentally revised first. Earlier rounds of digitisation, it notes, did not deliver the promised lower costs and smaller staff; they produced new tasks, more capacity and more hired-in workers.

Most of the saving is meant to come from doing less. AI Insider reports that general efficiency of at most 0.5 per cent a year, the figure used by the CPB, the government's economic forecaster, covers about a third of the target, and that the remainder must come from dropping tasks. By our arithmetic, 0.5 per cent a year compounded over the nine years from 2027 to 2035 comes to about 4.4 per cent of 160,016, or roughly 7,100 posts, a little short of a third of 25,000 (about 8,300). NOS lists the measures as leaner facilities at ministries, standardised IT systems and efficiency measures "including with AI", plus cuts to 28 advisory councils and about 150 advisory committees.

The baseline has moved the other way for years. Central government grew by about 41 per cent, or 46,500 full-time equivalents, between 2018 and 2025, according to the letter as AI Insider reports it, and Bloomberg put the number of officials at a record 160,000 at the end of last year. By our subtraction, a cut of 25,000 would leave about 135,000 posts, still roughly 21,500 more than in 2018. The €3.5bn target works out at about €140,000 a year for each post removed, by our arithmetic, which suggests the figure covers more than salaries alone.

Why it matters: governments are starting to book AI into budgets before they have measured it. The Dutch plan fixes the saving now and leaves the AI contribution to a later document, which means the technology's share will be judged against a target already set. Parliament's 20 per cent productivity motion and the letter's own caution now point in different directions, and the first-quarter AI plan will have to reconcile them. Mr van der Burg said he hoped to complete much of the detail within six months, NOS reported; the precise allocation is due in the spring budget memorandum, according to the letter as AI Insider reports it.

The Dutch state has moved carefully on technology before: its tax administration said this month it would run its mail on its own servers rather than finish a move to Microsoft 365, Komposite reported. In the United States, the federal government has taken the procurement route, ordering DeepSeek out of civilian agencies, while in the private sector Microsoft has offered buyouts to about 60,000 staff as it raises AI spending.
