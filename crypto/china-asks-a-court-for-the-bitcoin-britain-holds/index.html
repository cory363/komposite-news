China asks a court for the bitcoin Britain holds

Prosecutors in Tianjin have asked a court to confiscate nearly 62,000 bitcoin as the proceeds of a 40bn-yuan investment fraud run by Qian Zhimin. The coins sit in British custody, where a High Court ruling has just put victims' claims under Chinese law.

Marcus Oyelaran · House byline
October 22, 20262 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Chinese prosecutors have asked a court in Tianjin to confiscate nearly 62,000 bitcoin that British authorities seized from Qian Zhimin, a fugitive fraud suspect, Yicai Global reported, setting a Chinese claim against coins held in the UK. Yicai valued the bitcoin at about $5.1bn at mid-October prices.

The Tianjin No. 2 Intermediate People's Court said on 13 October that it had accepted the case on 28 September, according to Yicai's account of the court's announcement. CoinDesk reported, citing the Chinese outlet The Paper, that the court has opened a six-month period for victims and other interested parties to join. Investors who choose not to take part will keep their right to compensation from the recovered assets, the court said, according to Yicai.

Prosecutors allege that Qian, as the actual controller of Tianjin Lantian Gerui Electronic Technology, sold wealth-management products across China between April 2014 and August 2017 without a licence. The scheme raised nearly 40.4bn yuan from more than 102,000 investors, who lost over 19.9bn yuan, Yicai said. Yicai converted the total to about $6bn; CoinDesk put it at roughly $5.7bn. The prosecution says the money repaid earlier investors, paid for fake investment projects and bought bitcoin, property, cars and jewellery.

According to Yicai, Qian fled China in July 2017 with a computer holding a bitcoin wallet and later reached Britain. A district prosecutor in Tianjin approved her arrest in January 2018, and Interpol issued a Red Notice the following month. British authorities arrested her in April 2024, and she is now serving a prison sentence in the UK for cryptocurrency money laundering.

The assets at issue are the ones British authorities seized. The court's announcement lists more than 61,800 bitcoin, over 72,700 bitcoin cash and more than 3.6m XRP tokens, along with cash and jewellery, Yicai reported. Prosecutors argue that the proceeds, assets bought with them and any income they produced should be confiscated, except for amounts returned to victims under Chinese law.

The English courts have already narrowed the victims' route. The High Court in London ruled on Monday 12 October that Chinese law, not English law, governs victims' claims to the coins, Decrypt reported. Justice Mark Turner found that the fraud, the payments and the contracts all took place in China, and left the content of Chinese law to a later hearing. Fieldfisher, the law firm for the claimants, is pursuing an appeal, according to Decrypt, which cited the Financial Times.

The Crown Prosecution Service said: "There will be a further hearing to determine the application of the law and if any of the victims are able to establish an interest in the bitcoin." Prosecutors in Britain have previously said victims could be compensated through a scheme run from China, Yicai and Decrypt both reported.

Why it matters: the coins have risen far in value since Qian bought them, and the open question is who keeps that gain, whether victims, the Chinese state through confiscation, or Britain. A confiscation order from Tianjin would not by itself move coins held in Britain, and no Chinese ruling has yet been reported.
