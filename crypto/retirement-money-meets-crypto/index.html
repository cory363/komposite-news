Retirement money meets crypto

A proposed rule would give plan fiduciaries a process to follow rather than a warning to heed. It does not require anyone to offer anything.

Priya Raghavan · Markets Reporter
September 6, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

The largest pool of long-term savings in the country is held in workplace retirement plans, and the people who choose what those plans offer are governed less by opinion than by liability. That is the lever a proposed rule is aimed at.

Reporting on the proposal describes a framework that would make it easier for plans to include alternative assets, cryptocurrencies among them, alongside private equity and real estate.

What the rule actually creates is a process, not a product. It offers fiduciaries a defined path they can follow and document, and it does not require any plan to offer anything to anybody.

That distinction is the whole mechanism. A plan sponsor's reluctance was never mainly about volatility; it was about being sued for imprudence. Removing the ambiguity about what prudence requires is what changes behaviour.

The preceding step was quieter and equally consequential. Earlier guidance urging extreme caution about crypto in retirement plans was withdrawn, which removed a document that a plaintiff could have cited.

Critics have made a straightforward objection. A safe harbour built on process rather than outcome protects the fiduciary who follows the steps, and the participant carries the result regardless of whether the steps produced a good decision.

The counterargument is that this describes every investment option in every plan, and that singling out one asset class for a higher standard is a policy choice rather than a neutral application of existing law.

The practical effect in the near term is likely to be modest and indirect. Large sponsors move slowly, committees are conservative, and the first appearances are more likely inside diversified options than as a standalone fund on a menu.

That indirect route is where the real questions sit. An allocation held inside a target-date fund is one a participant did not choose and may not know about, which is a different consent question from an explicit option.

The proposal is not final and the process has further steps to run. What has already changed is the posture: the default answer moved from caution to procedure, and defaults are what actually decide where retirement money goes.
