Strategy sells common stock to buy back its preferred

Strategy raised $283.8m from new common shares last week and spent $188.3m of it buying back its STRC preferred stock, against $81.9m on bitcoin. By our tally of its filings, buybacks have outrun bitcoin purchases five to one since late September.

Marcus Oyelaran · House byline
November 9, 20264 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Two dollars in three that Strategy raised from new common shares last week went to buying back its own preferred stock, its Form 8-K filed on Monday shows. The company sold 2,051,348 class A shares for net proceeds of $283.8m between 2 and 8 November. It spent $188.3m repurchasing 1,887,464 shares of its STRC preferred, $81.9m on 1,036 bitcoin and put the remaining $13.6m into cash.

Strategy, formerly MicroStrategy, describes itself as the largest institutional holder of bitcoin. It held 850,364 coins as of Sunday, bought for $64.16bn at an average of $75,456 each, fees included. Its weekly filings usually lead with bitcoin bought with new money. Lately the larger figure has been in the repurchase table. STRC, which Strategy calls Stretch, is a perpetual preferred share with a variable dividend, and the company has been buying it back every week since the end of September.

How much has gone into buybacks?

By our tally, $1.16bn in six weeks. Komposite added up the repurchase and bitcoin tables in each weekly filing from 5 October to Monday's. Strategy bought back $1,163.2m of STRC over that stretch and bought 2,698 bitcoin for $219.7m, so the buybacks were about 5.3 times the bitcoin purchases. In one week, 19 to 25 October, it bought no bitcoin at all and spent $204.9m on STRC.

Why did the source of the money change?

Because the cash pile it had been using was nearly spent. Strategy keeps two pots of dollars: a USD Reserve, which it says is meant to cover preferred dividends and interest on its debt, and "USD Cash", which management can use for wider purposes including buybacks. Until last week the STRC repurchases came out of USD Cash, which fell from $833.4m on 4 October to $132.7m on 1 November, a drop of $700.7m by our subtraction. Last week was the first in the run in which the filing says new common shares paid for them. The reserve itself has barely moved: $4.77bn on Sunday, against $4.88bn in early October.

The board has twice made room for the purchases. On 19 October, Strategy said its directors had raised the authorisation under what it calls its digital credit securities repurchase programme from $2.0bn to $3.0bn. After last week, $560.3m of that remains, so roughly $2.44bn of the $3.0bn has been used, by our arithmetic. A separate $1.0bn authorisation to buy back MSTR common stock has not been touched.

What price is Strategy paying?

Just under $100 a share, every week. Dividing each week's aggregate purchase price by the shares repurchased gives averages between $99.06 and $99.76. That matters because STRC is meant to trade near $100. In a filing on 15 October, Strategy set the STRC dividend at 12.00 per cent a year for November and said management would recommend keeping it there "until STRC has demonstrated sustained, healthy trading near $100 per share". From 1 November the dividend is declared for every calendar day. The company has not said in these filings why it is buying the shares back, and the repurchases' effect on the price is not something the filings measure.

Where does that leave the bitcoin?

Below its cost, at Monday's price. Bitcoin traded at $74,812 at 14:58 GMT, according to CoinGecko, $644 below Strategy's average purchase price. At that price, by our arithmetic, the 850,364 coins were worth about $63.62bn, some $540m less than Strategy paid. Its last three purchases cost between $79,012 and $82,900 a coin, above the average, so each recent purchase has nudged the average up by a few dollars, from $75,446 on 18 October to $75,456 now. The common shares Strategy sold last week went for an average of $138.35 net, by our division, against $139.15 the week before.

Why it matters: Strategy's model depends on selling securities to investors who want exposure to bitcoin, and its preferred shares are the part of that machine that promises a fixed-looking income. When the company sells common stock to support the price of its preferred, it is moving value between its own classes of investor rather than adding bitcoin. Holders of MSTR are absorbing the dilution, and holders of STRC are getting a buyer near par. Neither shows up in the bitcoin count. Bitcoin holders elsewhere have had their own reminders of where the price thresholds sit, as Komposite found when Bitget's user fund was measured in coins rather than dollars.

Strategy has filed these updates on Mondays, which would make the next one due on 16 November. It will show whether the buybacks continue with $560.3m of authorisation left, and whether last week's switch to new common shares was a one-off. Strategy is not the only company leaning on preferred stock this autumn: Meta sold $12.5bn of mandatory convertible preferred alongside its common stock last month, as Komposite reported. That money was raised, not spent retiring an earlier issue.
