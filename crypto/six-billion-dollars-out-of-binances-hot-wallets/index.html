Six billion dollars out of Binance's hot wallets

Binance says about $6.3bn was taken from its hot wallets on Thursday, the largest crypto theft on record, and that it will cover every loss. Stablecoin issuers have frozen about $2.7bn. TRM Labs attributes the theft to North Korea.

Marcus Oyelaran · House byline
October 30, 20264 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

About $6.3bn in crypto was taken from Binance, the world's largest exchange, on Thursday in what is the largest theft in the industry's history, the company said in an update on Friday. Binance said it would cover all losses, that "user funds are safe" and that withdrawals were suspended while it completed security checks.

Binance's update says an initial investigation suggests "a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop" allowed an attacker to reach a service container and submit unauthorised withdrawal transactions. "We have found no evidence of any wallet private key leak," it says. The exchange said about $3bn had been frozen on-chain with partners, that "Binance will cover all losses from the SAFU fund and the company's funds", referring to its emergency reserve, and that it would publish a technical post-mortem and an updated proof of reserves. Deposits are unaffected. The update gives no date for withdrawals to resume.

TRM Labs, a blockchain analytics firm, counts $6.36bn moved out of Binance wallets on ten blockchains, most of it in about 20 minutes from 20:05 UTC on Thursday. It attributes the theft "with high confidence" to TraderTraitor, a North Korean unit that the FBI blamed for the theft of about $1.5bn from the exchange Bybit in February 2025. Ari Redbord, TRM's global head of policy, called the attack "unprecedented", Al Jazeera reported. Binance has not named a culprit; it said it was working with law enforcement.

The two accounts of how it happened are not yet the same. Binance points to one engineer's laptop. TRM says the attacker produced valid signatures on chains with different key types and signing schemes within about half an hour, which "a simple phishing explanation" does not cover, and that on Ethereum it used 50 pre-signed authorisations under EIP-7702, a 2025 feature that lets an ordinary wallet hand control to a smart contract, prepared so that one would match whatever the hot wallet's transaction count was at the time. TRM's assessment is that the attacker could direct Binance's hot-wallet signing system across chains. Binance has not published a root cause.

The freezes came quickly. Tether froze 971.1m USDT 37 minutes after the main sweep and Circle froze 823m USDC three minutes later, working with law enforcement, according to TRM. The attacker still controls about $2.4bn, including more than 420,000 ether and the 18,270 bitcoin, which it is moving through a chain of small transfers. TRM says it sold about 325m USDT to market makers for roughly 122,300 ether at an implied price of about $2,650, when ether traded at $2,300 to $2,350, giving up more than 10 per cent to convert tokens that could be frozen into one that cannot.

By our arithmetic, the theft is 4.2 times the Bybit loss and 2.2 times the $2.87bn that TRM says was stolen in nearly 150 crypto hacks in the whole of 2025. Binance told ABC News it had reserved more than $6.34bn of company funds for the incident, $5bn of it in the same tokens that were taken, which would cover the gross loss before any recovery. That figure is the company's own and has not been independently verified. Bitcoin fell about 5 per cent to below $77,000 after the news, Al Jazeera reported.

Binance confirmed the incident to TechCrunch on Thursday after users reported funds draining from its wallets; hours earlier Changpeng Zhao, the founder and largest shareholder, who no longer runs the exchange, had posted that it had probably been hacked. Sean Foley of Macquarie University told Al Jazeera: "Any assets held at a centralised exchange are only as safe as the exchange itself."

Why it matters: by our arithmetic from TRM's breakdown, about $3.4bn of the stolen value, more than half, was in dollar stablecoins, and those were the easiest to stop because their issuers can freeze them; nearly all of the $2.7bn frozen so far is stablecoins. The ether and bitcoin cannot be frozen that way. Hacken, a security firm, had already attributed most third-quarter crypto hack losses to suppliers, unapplied fixes and systems left out of security reviews, as Komposite reported this month, rather than to flaws in audited code. Binance accounts have also figured in American enforcement: prosecutors were seeking to seize $61m from two of them in September.
