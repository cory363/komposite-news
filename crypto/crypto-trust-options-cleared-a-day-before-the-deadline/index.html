Crypto-trust options cleared a day before the deadline

The SEC on Tuesday approved Nasdaq ISE's rule for listing options on trusts that hold digital commodities, one day before its own 11 November deadline and five days after the exchange rewrote the filing. Nobody commented. Our timeline and reading of what changed.

Marcus Oyelaran · House byline
November 11, 20265 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

The Securities and Exchange Commission had until Wednesday to decide what to do with Nasdaq ISE's proposal to list options on trusts that hold digital commodities. It acted on Tuesday. Release No. 34-106863, dated 10 November 2026, approves the rule change on an accelerated basis, 105 days after the exchange filed it on 28 July, by our count.

In practical terms, the order lets ISE list options on shares of commodity-based trusts that Nasdaq lists under its generic standards, provided each trust passes a liquidity test and a surveillance test, without a separate SEC filing for each new option. It does not approve options on any named product. "The Commission received no comments regarding the proposed rule change," the order records, the same finding as when we first followed the filing in September.

The order was not signed by the commissioners. It was issued "for the Commission, by the Division of Trading and Markets, pursuant to delegated authority", over the name of Sherry R. Haywood, assistant secretary. That is routine for exchange rule filings, and it matters more than usual at a commission that fell to two members in October, when it changed its rules so that one commissioner can be a quorum in some matters.

How long did it take?

Fifteen weeks, most of it waiting. The filing sat for 98 days before the exchange amended it, then changed shape twice in a week. On 3 November ISE filed Amendment No. 1, replacing the whole proposal. Two days later it withdrew that and filed Amendment No. 2, which again replaced everything. The SEC approved the second version five days after that.

The 11 November date was not fixed by statute. It was the end of the longer period the commission gave itself in September, when the 45-day clock would otherwise have expired on a Sunday. As we wrote then, the deadline belonged to this options filing, not to any spot exchange-traded product. The commission could have approved, disapproved or opened proceedings. Opening proceedings would have extended the review, which the statute allows to run to 240 days from publication; it chose approval.

What did Amendment No. 2 change?

Mostly the paperwork around the rule rather than the rule. The order lists eight revisions plus non-substantive edits. ISE now promises to refile if Congress enacts a statutory definition of "digital commodity", says derivatives held by a trust count at gross notional value towards the 15 per cent limit, says it can obtain information from Nasdaq when investigating suspected manipulation, describes continued-listing requirements and surveillance procedures, and represents that it and the Options Price Reporting Authority have the systems capacity for the extra traffic. One revision corrects a grammatical error. The commission found that the amendment "raises no novel regulatory issues that have not previously been subject to comment".

The substance sits in three tests. The first is a change of vocabulary. ISE's rule used to allow options on trusts holding a "crypto asset", a definition written around the technology. The new rule uses "digital commodity", borrowed from Nasdaq's listing rule and informed by the joint SEC and CFTC interpretation that took effect on 23 March, which named bitcoin, ether and 16 other digital commodities. "Not every crypto asset qualifies as a digital commodity," the exchange wrote.

The liquidity floor does not move. Each holding's total global supply must have averaged at least $700m of daily market value over the previous 12 months, and that includes the holdings in the 15 per cent buffer. What does move is surveillance. Today every holding must underlie a derivatives contract on a market with which ISE shares surveillance information, directly or through the Intermarket Surveillance Group. Under the new rule, only 85 per cent of a trust's net asset value has to meet that test. The exchange argued that manipulating up to 15 per cent of a trust's assets is unlikely to move its net asset value or the price of the options.

For issuers, the order closes a gap that opened in July. ISE already had a generic standard for options on crypto trusts, but it required every holding to pass the surveillance test. Nasdaq's listing rule for commodity-based trusts, with the 15 per cent buffer and the digital-commodity definition, was approved on 27 July. A trust that used the buffer could list on Nasdaq from then but could not meet ISE's options test. The matching options rule took another 106 days.

Why it matters: options are how many institutions hedge and how many retail investors lever a position, and the options standard now matches the listing standard, so a trust that qualifies to list on Nasdaq's generic terms can also qualify for options on ISE. The next concrete step is the comment period on Amendment No. 2, which runs for 21 days after the notice is published in the Federal Register, and the docket for SR-ISE-2026-42 is where any comments, and any petition for the full commission to review a staff decision, would appear.
