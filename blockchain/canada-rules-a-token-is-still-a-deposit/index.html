Canada rules that a token is still a deposit

The supervisor says the technology used to record a deposit does not change what it legally is, which removes the question banks had been waiting on.

Marcus Oyelaran · Blockchain & Digital Assets Editor
September 12, 20262 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Banks have spent three years asking regulators a question that sounds technical and is not: if a deposit is recorded on a blockchain rather than in a ledger database, is it still a deposit? Canada has answered.

The Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions issued a statement on September 10 confirming that the technology used to represent a deposit does not change its legal nature. OSFI's formulation was direct: tokenized deposits are, for example, not legally distinct from traditional deposits.

The reasoning matters more than the conclusion. OSFI describes its approach as technology-neutral, meaning it asks what a financial product actually is rather than what it is built on. A deposit recorded through a blockchain remains a deposit and a liability of the issuing institution.

That sentence does the work. A bank deposit is a claim on the bank, protected and supervised as such. If tokenisation had created a legally distinct instrument, every protection attached to the original would have needed to be re-established for the new one, and the industry would have waited years for that.

The clarification is not a licence. OSFI said federally regulated institutions must continue to follow existing rules on technology, cybersecurity and third-party risk management, which is where a tokenised product actually gets examined.

Those three headings are not incidental. A deposit moved on a distributed ledger involves new software, new operational dependencies and usually a vendor who did not exist in the bank's risk register five years ago. The legal question is settled; the operational one is where supervision will land.

For Canadian banks the practical effect is that a product can now be designed against a known standard rather than against a guess. Uncertainty of this kind is expensive in a specific way: it does not stop work, it stops approval, and projects sit finished and unlaunched while counsel waits for a view.

The position also sets up a contrast worth watching. A deposit token issued by a bank and a stablecoin issued by a non-bank can look similar to a user and are entirely different instruments underneath. Saying so plainly makes the distinction easier to supervise, not harder.

Other supervisors have reached similar conclusions in fragments, through no-objection letters and pilot approvals. What is different here is that the statement is general rather than case-by-case, which is what allows a bank to plan rather than apply.

The limitation is jurisdictional. An institution operating across borders still faces a patchwork, and a deposit token that is unambiguous in Toronto may not be in another market. Canada has removed its own ambiguity, not anyone else's.

What the statement really signals is a regulator declining to be impressed. Treating a tokenised deposit as a deposit is the least dramatic possible response to the technology, and for the institutions that have to build on it, that is the point.
