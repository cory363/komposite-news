Private Credit Moves Onchain

The largest category of tokenised real-world assets is not Treasuries or funds. It is loans, and it is the hardest one to verify.

Marcus Oyelaran · Blockchain & Digital Assets Editor
September 6, 2026 · 2 min read
ShareXLinkedInBlueskyFacebookEmail

Tokenised real-world assets are usually discussed as Treasuries and money market funds, because those are easy to describe and easy to verify. The larger and more interesting category is private credit.

Analysis of the numbers behind the growth describes on-chain private credit expanding rapidly and being harder to count than other categories, because issuance runs across platforms with different reporting conventions.

That measurement difficulty is not incidental. It is the same property that makes private credit private: the loans are bilateral, the terms are negotiated, and there is no public market marking them.

Putting such a loan on a chain changes the settlement and the transfer, and it does not change the valuation. The token moves instantly and still represents an instrument whose worth is an opinion held by the originator.

That gap is where the enthusiasm and the risk sit together. Tokenisation genuinely improves transferability and administration for an asset class notorious for both, and it does nothing for the credit question underneath.

A buyer receiving a tokenised loan interest still has to answer the same things a buyer of a fund interest would: who underwrote it, what the recovery assumptions are, and who marks it. Those answers are not on the chain.

Institutional interest is nonetheless real and increasing, and surveys of asset managers report rising intent to tokenise their own assets, which is a statement about operations rather than about credit.

Operations is the honest case for the whole exercise. Settlement, register maintenance and collateral mobility are genuinely improved, and they are unglamorous enough that they get sold as something more transformative.

The failure mode to watch is a familiar one wearing new infrastructure. An illiquid asset made technically transferable can trade actively while its underlying value is unobservable, which is precisely the arrangement that goes wrong slowly and then quickly.

The instrument is not the innovation. The register is, and the register is only as good as whoever is entitled to write to it.
